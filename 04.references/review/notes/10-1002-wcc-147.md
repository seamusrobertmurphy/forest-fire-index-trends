Subject. The text reviewed indices of temperature and precipitation extremes from daily station data, chiefly the 27 agreed by the Expert Team on Climate Change Detection and Indices.

Establishes. The review, an Advanced Review of 2011, set out how the 27 internationally agreed indices of daily temperature and precipitation extremes were defined and computed (PDF p. 4). It traced a shift from fixed, physically based thresholds, few of which suited every part of the world, to relative thresholds in the tails of each variable's distribution (PDF p. 1). A relative threshold was a percentile of a station's own base period climate, so the count above it sampled the same part of the distribution everywhere (PDF pp. 4–6). For temperature, the threshold was set for each calendar day from a five-day window in the base period, to allow for the mean annual cycle (PDF pp. 4, 7). For precipitation, it was set from all wet days in the base period, without regard to the annual cycle (PDF p. 5). The base period was fixed to a common period, 1961 to 1990 in the examples, so that stations with different record lengths could be compared and updated (PDF p. 7). Inside the base period, exceedance of a 90th percentile threshold was 10 per cent by definition (PDF p. 7). Outside it, sampling error and a positive bias at high quantiles changed the rate, which put steps into annual counts at both ends of the base period (PDF p. 7). The steps grew with the rarity of the threshold and could be large enough to affect trends, most of all in regional averages (PDF p. 7). The review summarised the 2005 bootstrap correction of Zhang, Hegerl and Zwiers, which withheld each base period year from the threshold applied to that year (PDF p. 7). Its Figure 3 showed counts above the 90th and 99th percentiles averaged over 210 Canadian stations for 1900 to 2000 (PDF p. 8). Over the in-base years, the corrected counts were higher than the uncorrected ones, most of all at the 99th percentile. Where data precision was coarse against daily variability, counting values strictly above a 90th percentile gave a rate below 10 per cent, and counting ties gave one above (PDF p. 8). It noted that most homogeneity methods did not consider changes in how a series was derived (PDF p. 6). The review computed no new trends, and it never mentioned fire weather or fire danger indices. It listed wind speed and humidity among the variables that future indices might use (PDF pp. 15–16).

Manuscript sentences checked

1. qmd L2211, "Percentile thresholds move with the series [@zhang2011indices]". Verdict, does not hold. The idea behind the cell has support, but the wording is not the text's and can be read as the opposite of what the text said. Evidence, the text never said a threshold moved. It set thresholds "relative to a base period climate" (PDF p. 4), over a base period "fixed to a common period (such as 1961–1990)" (PDF p. 7). It valued percentile thresholds for spatial comparison because "they sample the same part of the probability distribution of" (PDF p. 5) "temperature and precipitation at each location." (PDF p. 6). A threshold fixed this way did not follow a series that changed after its base period. For one model's simulated climate of 2070 to 2099, "the 90th percentile computed from the 1961–1990 base period was exceeded 70% of the time in some regions." (PDF p. 15). Read as a threshold that changed through time, the phrase contradicts the text. Read as each series carrying its own threshold, it moves the text's argument from stations to versions of one index, which the text never discussed. Proposed wording for the cell's first item, Counts above percentile thresholds "sample the same part of the probability distribution" at each location, with the base-period crossing rate fixed [@zhang2011indices, p. 5]. The page is a PDF page of the early view, which printed none. The version of record ran 851 to 870, the same 20 pages, so PDF p. 5 is probably printed p. 855, unconfirmed here.

2. qmd L2574, "Relative thresholds followed @zhang2011indices." Verdict, holds but the citation should give a page. It also leaves out a departure from the text's method that a reader familiar with the indices would not expect. Evidence, the text described thresholds "relative to a base period climate" (PDF p. 4) and a base period "fixed to a common period (such as 1961–1990)" (PDF p. 7). Its crossing rate was fixed "during the base period, such as 10% of all recorded days" (PDF p. 5). For a 90th percentile, rates inside the base period "are by definition exactly 10%" (PDF p. 7), which supports the paragraph's ten per cent sentence. The departure is the annual cycle. For temperature, the text's thresholds were "calculated from five-day windows centered on each calendar day to account for the mean annual cycle." (PDF p. 4). The paragraph sets one distribution, so one threshold, per station and series. That matches the text's precipitation thresholds, "calculated from the sample of all wet days in the base period without consideration of the annual cycle." (PDF p. 5). A referee could quote the text's warning that thresholds pooled across seasons make exceedance changes "largely reflect mean winter and summer temperature changes" (PDF p. 6). Four parts do not rest on this text. The 1991 to 2020 period and the clause "the current World Meteorological Organization normal" appear nowhere in the text, whose examples used 1961 to 1990 and which never mentioned climate normals. That clause needs its own source. The 95th percentile of the Initial Spread Index rests on @richardson2022global, as the paragraph says. The text never mentioned fire weather or fire danger indices, so applying the method to the Fire Weather Index and the Initial Spread Index was the study's own step. The paragraph as given names no in-base correction. Where the Methods state one, its source is @zhang2005percentile, the text's reference 51, which the text summarised on PDF p. 7. Proposed wording, replacing the first sentence, inserting one sentence after the fourth and giving the fifth a comma and a page.
"Relative thresholds were percentiles of a base period climate, as in the internationally agreed indices of climate extremes [@zhang2011indices, pp. 4, 7]. They were the 90th and 95th percentiles of each series' own Fire Weather Index distribution, and the 95th percentile of its Initial Spread Index distribution. The last was the fire weather day threshold of @richardson2022global applied to the spread index. Each was set at each station over the 1991 to 2020 reference period, the current World Meteorological Organization normal. Each threshold covered the whole season, as in the agreed precipitation indices, whereas the agreed temperature indices set one for each calendar day [@zhang2011indices, pp. 4–5]. Inside the reference period, the mean count above the 90th percentile was near ten per cent of days in every series by construction [@zhang2011indices, p. 7]. Only the trend of a relative count therefore said anything about the version."
The inserted sentence restates the study's design from the paragraph's wording and should be checked against the code. The pages are PDF pages of the early view, which printed none, and PDF p. N is probably printed p. 850 plus N, unconfirmed here.
