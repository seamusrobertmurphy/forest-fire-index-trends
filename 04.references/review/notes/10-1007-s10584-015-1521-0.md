Question Q2, because it measures how a change in the weather input, temperature and precipitation perturbed on a fixed station record, moves the level and the tail of the fuel moisture codes, which is the mechanism behind any input-driven shift in the index; and Q1 in passing, since Kamloops is one of its twelve stations and it sets the FFMC 91 threshold the manuscript counts.

Establishes Flannigan, Wotton, Marshall, de Groot, Johnston, Jurko and Cantin, Climatic Change 134, 59 to 71, took the 1200 local standard time weather of twelve Canadian fire weather stations, Kamloops among them, for the fire seasons of 1971 to 2000, built 54 modified weather streams by adding 0 to 5 degrees to daily temperature and scaling daily precipitation by minus 40 to plus 40 per cent, and recomputed the Fine Fuel Moisture Code, Duff Moisture Code and Drought Code of the Fire Weather Index System for June to August of each. Precipitation had to rise by about 15 per cent per degree for the FFMC, 10 per cent for the DMC and 5 per cent for the DC to hold the mean code unchanged, and for the count of days at or above FFMC 91, the spread threshold, no precipitation increase up to 40 per cent compensated for a single degree of warming, the percentage of such days rising from 7.69 at the baseline to 8.64 at plus 1 degree and 13.32 at plus 5 degrees with no precipitation change. The paper held relative humidity and wind fixed, so it understated the drying, and changed precipitation amount but not frequency. It was a sensitivity experiment on a fixed record, not a trend study; it computed no observed trend, no occurrence model and no comparison of index versions.

Quotations

"We obtained weather data from 12 stations across Canada for the fire season during the 1971–2000 period and with these data we created a set of modified weather streams from the original data by varying the daily temperatures by 0 to +5 °C in increments of 1 °C and the daily precipitation from −40 to 40 % in increments of 10 %." (p. 59)

"When temperature increases we find that for every degree of warming, precipitation has to increase by more than 15 % for FFMC, about 10 % for DMC and about 5 % for DC to compensate for the drying caused by warmer temperatures. Also, we find in terms of the number of days equal to or above an FFMC of 91, a critical value for fire spread, that no increase in precipitation amount alone could compensate for a temperature increase of 1 °C." (p. 59)

"The variables obtained are those used to calculate components of the Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index (FWI) System (Van Wagner 1987) namely 1200 local standard time (LST) temperature, relative humidity, wind speed (measured at 10 m above ground in the open) and 24 h accumulated precipitation (Lawson and Armitage 2008)." (p. 61)

"The drying time lags (the time it takes for fuel to move ~63 % of the way toward equilibrium moisture content) for these three fuel layers are 2/3 of a day, 15 and 52 days respectively for the FFMC, DMC and DC under normal conditions (temperature 21.1 °C, relative humidity 45 %) (Van Wagner 1987)." (p. 62)

"We examined the number of days where FFMC values exceeded 84 and 91 (corresponding to roughly to 17 and 10 % moisture content in pine litter respectively), which represent reasonable thresholds for fire ignition and vigorous fire spread respectively (Wotton 2009)." (p. 62)

"Overall these summarized frequencies show similar results to the change in means of the distributions shown in Figs. 1, 2 and 3, although rainfall increases needed to compensate for rising temperatures tend to be higher. Table 2 (the FFMC=91 threshold, indicating strong fire spread potential) shows the relatively muted effect that changes in rainfall have on the number of days hitting this threshold. Not even a 40 % increase in precipitation amount could compensate for an increase in temperature of a degree or more." (p. 64)

"The lower moisture content associated with the higher temperatures tested is likely a conservative estimate of increased drying as we have not included the influence of the decreased relative humidity that would accompany an increase in air temperature during a rain-free period; thus it is likely that in reality to maintain current average fuel moisture levels even greater increases in rainfall would be needed to offset the overall effects atmospheric warming." (p. 66)

"We did not change the relative humidity or wind speed and these variables can influence fuel moisture. The influence of wind on drying is relatively minor in the FFMC and is not included in the drying of the sub-surface layers tracked by the DMC." (pp. 67 to 68)

"Indeed sensitivity studies on weather inputs and outputs of the FWI System have shown there to be strong influence of relative humidity (Dowdy et al. 2010)." (p. 68)

"Precipitation frequency is unchanged so this study addresses sensitivity of precipitation amounts but not the sensitivity to precipitation frequency." (p. 68)
