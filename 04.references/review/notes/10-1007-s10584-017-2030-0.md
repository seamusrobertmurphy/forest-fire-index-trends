Question Q1, because it is a Canadian study of change in Fire Weather Index System extremes that reports a modelled shift in the fire season 90th percentile rather than an observed station trend, and so marks what the Canadian record has and has not been used for; Q2 in passing, for its noon input standard and its note on the difference between station and gridded values.

Establishes Kirchmeier-Young, Zwiers, Gillett and Cannon, Climatic Change 144, 365 to 379, quantified the influence of anthropogenic forcing on extreme fire seasons in the homogeneous fire regime zone of northern Alberta that holds Fort McMurray, 267 grid boxes of about 5.7 million hectares, by event attribution with two 50-member CanESM2 large ensembles, one with all forcings and one with natural forcings only, downscaled and bias corrected against the Global Fire Weather Database for 1980 to 2014 with Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation. Fourteen metrics were defined from the Fire Weather Index System and the Fire Behaviour Prediction System, the main ones the fire season 90th percentile of each code exceeding the Natural Resources Canada extreme thresholds of FFMC 91, DMC 60, DC 425, ISI 15, BUI 90 and FWI 30, with spread days defined after Wang et al. (2014) as FWI of 19 or more with DMC of 20 or more. Probabilities were pooled over 500 model years for 2011 to 2020 and the risk ratio, probability of necessary causality and probability of sufficient causality computed with bootstrap intervals. For most metrics all forcings made extreme fire seasons 1.5 to 6 times as likely, the risk ratio for a fire season 90th percentile FWI above 30 being 5.97 and for a season over 165 days 4.12. The observed record entered only as the bias-correction target and as a density comparison of FWI on large-fire days against all days; the paper estimated no observed trend at any station or grid box, used no Theil-Sen or Mann-Kendall estimator and no multiple-testing correction across its fourteen metrics, and its attributed warming of about 1 degree over 1980 to 2014 came from a detection and attribution regression on temperature, not on the indices.

"Fourteen metrics from the Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System are used to define the extreme fire seasons. For the majority of these metrics and during the current decade, the combined effect of anthropogenic and natural forcing is estimated to have made extreme fire risk events in the region 1.5 to 6 times as likely compared to a climate that would have been with natural forcings alone." (p. 365)

"GFWED provides the four main FWI System indices as well as the daily input weather data for the FWI-system standard of local noon values. The temperature, relative humidity (RH), and wind speed inputs for the FWI indices calculated here are from GFWED." (p. 367)

"The FWI system indices are calculated daily using observations at local noon and depend on the previous day’s index. We use the recommended values (NRCan 2016) to initiate the calculations on the first day of the fire season and ignore overwintering adjustments." (p. 369)

"The maximum FWI in the first four days following ignition is assigned to each fire; this window was chosen based on when a fire consumes most of its fuel (Amiro et al. 2004). In general, large fires occur on days with greater fire potential (larger FWI). There are many days every year that experienced extreme fire risk but either lacked an ignition source or any ignited fires were suppressed quickly." (p. 369)

"After scaling the model response to be consistent with the observations, the result is an attributed warming trend of about 1 ◦C over the period for which the FWI indices can be calculated (1980-2014)." (p. 372)

"First, we define a class of events for each FWI index by requiring the 90th percentile of daily index values for each fire season to exceed an NRCan (2016) defined “extreme” threshold." (p. 372)

"Bearing in mind the inherent difference between station and gridded observations, these values correspond, respectively, to the >99, 94, 89, >99, 95, >99th percentiles in the corresponding grid box of GFWED data." (p. 373)

"We also considered events defined in terms of days with significant spread potential, by using the 90th percentile value of the ROS (Rate of Spread) and also the definition of Wang et al.
