Subject. Fire Weather Index System codes computed from raw and from homogenised daily weather at 16 Canadian stations, compared in value, distribution and trend.

Establishes. The paper defined inhomogeneity as any change at a station site, including landscape change, urbanisation, irrigation, instruments, observation procedure, relocation and the surrounding environment. It called station relocation the most disruptive change, and its nationwide Canadian examples included moves to airports in the 1940s and a new climatological day in 1961. Environment and Climate Change Canada supplied raw records for 16 stations, with its own homogenised daily temperature, daily precipitation and monthly wind. The authors homogenised daily wind and relative humidity themselves with RHTest V4, which found breaks by penalised maximal t and F tests and adjusted them by quantile matching. Most relative humidity records had no more than one break. The breaks mostly fell in the 1970s to 1990s, the years of the switch to dewcel instruments at stations with metadata. The stations formed three sets, none of them continuous in time. Dataset 1 held all 16 stations over 27 fire seasons from 1965 to 2005, and Dataset 2 held 10 stations over 49 seasons from 1956 to 2008. Dataset 3 held five stations, Kapuskasing, Fort Smith, Mayo, Terrace and Quesnel, over 66 seasons from 1926 to 2001. Table 1 gave early temperature and precipitation start years only for those five stations, for a separate long Drought Code dataset. The archive held no wind or relative humidity before the 1950s, and Table 6 reported only temperature, precipitation and the Drought Code for Dataset 3. The codes came from the equations and FORTRAN program of Van Wagner and Pickett (1985), the version this manuscript labels FWI1987. The standard inputs were read at 12:00 local standard time or 13:00 local daylight time, but the homogenised data held no noon values. Both runs therefore used daily values, with mean temperature taken as the average of the maximum and minimum and precipitation summed over a climatological day. Daily mean temperature ran about 0.4 to 2.0 °C below noon temperature, and the maximum about 3.0 to 5.0 °C above it. Each season ran from 1 April to the end of September, and the Drought Code restarted at 15 every spring. Homogenised series were warmer and wetter at every Dataset 2 station except Timmins, and the indices computed from them were lower overall. In Table 5, for Dataset 2, the mean raw minus homogenised Drought Code difference was positive at all 10 stations, from 6.06 to 10.6. The mean difference was negative at Fort Saint John for every other code, and at Terrace for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code, Fire Weather Index and Daily Severity Rating. The text nonetheless called the mean difference persistently positive for the Fire Weather Index and Daily Severity Rating. The mean Daily Severity Rating fell about 4 per cent. The other indices fell 0.7 to 8.4 per cent, least for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and most for the Drought Code. No table printed the index means behind these percentages, so the range could not be recomputed from the article. The abstract tied the range to the 66 season data, but the Results gave it after Table 5, which held Dataset 2. There, the mean Daily Severity Rating difference of about 0.14 matched the mean of the absolute Table 5 values, 0.136. The Fine Fuel Moisture Code, named as the low end, was not reported for Dataset 3. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test rejected the null hypothesis of a difference symmetric about zero at most stations, and at all of them with 66 season records. By that test, the Drought Code was affected at every station in all three sets, and the Duff Moisture Code at 60 to 80 per cent. Mann-Kendall and linear regression tests found the linear trend changed at up to 60 per cent of stations for some codes, with tables only in Tsinko (2016). The article gave no count of reversed trends and no test statistic for any reversal. Kapuskasing was the one named case, with a falling raw trend and a rising homogenised trend for the Drought Code and Fire Weather Index. Fig. 6, its caption and the text disagreed on the index, the statistic and the years. The text called it the median of the Fire Weather Index and Drought Code, but the caption described average Drought Code values for 1918 to 1998. The upper panel, labelled as fire season Fire Weather Index, began in the 1920s, before the archive held wind or humidity. On the page image, the raw trend line fell in both panels, and the homogenised line rose, clearly in the upper panel and slightly in the lower. The authors judged overall trends subtle and possibly insignificant, though a change from a negative to a positive trend was possibly meaningful. The effect appeared larger, and likely biased, in records before 1960, and homogenised values often missed the peaks of recent extreme years. Homogenisation could remove extreme heat wave events in part or entirely, and the root mean square showed changes 10 times larger for outliers. At Fort Smith on 31 August 2009, homogenised inputs raised the Fire Weather Index from 13.914 to 16.469. The Daily Severity Rating rose from 2.874 to 3.8713, from the moderate to the high class.

Bears on

- Introduction, "In Canada at 16 stations, 66-year weather records were homogenised, which removed the breaks a change of instrument or practice had imposed [@tsinko2018comparisons]." (qmd L2194). Contradicts. Sixteen stations were used, but "the third set included only five stations with the longest time period of 66 years" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 110). The other sets held all 16 stations over 27 seasons and 10 stations over 49. The 66 seasons ran from 1926 to 2001 with gaps, by Table 2 (PDF p. 3, printed p. 112). They were temperature and precipitation records "used for a separate long DC dataset", and the archive held no wind or humidity before the 1950s (PDF p. 3, printed p. 112). Environment and Climate Change Canada had homogenised the daily temperature and precipitation (PDF p. 4, printed p. 113). The authors homogenised daily wind and relative humidity with RHTest V4, by penalised maximal t and F tests and quantile matching (PDF p. 4, printed p. 113). On cause, the abstract named "Modifications to the environment around a weather station or changes in instrument" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 110). The Introduction added observation procedure and station relocation, the most disruptive change (PDF p. 1, printed p. 110). On effect, the Conclusion said the techniques "reduce the inhomogeneity in weather station data" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 117), not that they removed the breaks. A corrected sentence would read, "In Canada, daily records from 16 weather stations were homogenised to adjust breaks from changes of instrument, observation procedure, station site and surroundings [@tsinko2018comparisons]." If the record length is kept, a second sentence would read, "Five of the stations carried temperature and precipitation over 66 fire seasons between 1926 and 2001 [@tsinko2018comparisons]."
- Introduction, "Homogenising lowered the fire danger indices computed from them by about 0.7 to 8.4 per cent and reversed the long-term trend at some stations [@tsinko2018comparisons]." (qmd L2194). Qualifies. The range and the direction were printed, "Homogenization of 66 years data reduced the indices values approximately 0.7–8.4%" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 110). The Results gave the range after Table 5, which held the 49 season Dataset 2, as "The rest of the indices are reduced from 0.7% to 8.4%, with the lowest changes in FFMC and highest changes in DC" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 117). "The rest" left out the Daily Severity Rating, whose average fell about 4 per cent (PDF pp. 7 to 8, printed pp. 116 to 117). The Fine Fuel Moisture Code was not reported for the 66 season set, so the range did not come from those records alone, despite the abstract. The fall was an overall one, "Overall, CFFWI indices driven by homogenized data are lower than the inhomogenized indices (Table 5)" (PDF p. 6, printed p. 115). In Table 5, homogenised means were higher at Fort Saint John and Terrace for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code, Fire Weather Index and Daily Severity Rating (PDF p. 7, printed p. 116). On trends, the abstract said "also reversed the long-term trend in some stations such as Kapuskasing" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 110). No count of reversing stations was given, and Kapuskasing was the only one named. There, "The inhomogenized observations show a decreasing trend at Kapuskasing, while the homogenized trend increases for DC and FWI indices" (PDF p. 7, printed p. 116). Fig. 6 drew linear trend lines with no test statistic, and its caption described Drought Code values only (PDF p. 9, printed p. 118). By "Mann Kendall and linear regression trend analysis tests", homogenisation changed the linear trend at "up to 60% of the stations affected in some codes" (PDF p. 5, printed p. 114). The authors added "Overall trends appear subtle, and may be insignificant" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 117). A corrected sentence would read, "Homogenising lowered the indices by 0.7 to 8.4 per cent, least for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and most for the Drought Code [@tsinko2018comparisons]." The trend clause would become, "At Kapuskasing, Ontario, the Drought Code showed a falling linear trend in the raw record and a rising one in the homogenised record [@tsinko2018comparisons]."
- Introduction, "Homogenising wind speed at 117 Canadian stations over 1953 to 2006 changed the trends, because the anemometer height had changed at 114 of them [@wan2010homogenization]." (qmd L2194). Adds to it at second hand. The paper reported that "97% of stations analyzed by Wan et al. (2010) experienced at least one type of discontinuity in wind speed records" (PDF p. 2, printed p. 111). That matched 114 of 117, or 97.4 per cent, but named no cause, so the anemometer height rests on Wan and others alone.
- Discussion, "Joined across the change, such a series would carry a step at the join, which a fitted line would read as trend." (qmd L3576). Adds to it. Adjusting breaks within the Kapuskasing record turned a falling Drought Code trend into a rising one (PDF p. 7, printed p. 116). The adjustment was largest before 1960, "Homogenization of older records (pre-1960) appears a more significant impact (likely biased), compared to newer records" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 117).
- Discussion, "A level shift that did not alter the relative change had been reported before in this index." (qmd L3578). Adds a contrast. In this paper, the adjustment was larger before 1960 than after, so it was not uniform through time (PDF p. 8, printed p. 117). It lowered the level and also changed the linear trend at up to 60 per cent of stations for some codes (PDF p. 5, printed p. 114).
- Limitations, "The sensor history of the stations was not available." (qmd L3619). Adds to it. At the 16 national stations, relative humidity breaks mostly "occurred in 1970–1990s and corresponded to the years of instrumentation switch to dewcel for stations with available metadata" (PDF p. 4, printed p. 113). The Okanagan stations belonged to BC Wildfire Service, so the finding applies by analogy only.
- Methods, "The Drought Code was not overwintered, that is carried over the winter into the next spring, because the stations reported no winter weather." (qmd L2558). Adds to it. The paper also restarted the Drought Code at 15 each spring. It judged that reasonable in Canada except "where total precipitation from November to April is lower than 200 mm (Girardin and Wotton, 2009)" (PDF p. 5, printed p. 114).
