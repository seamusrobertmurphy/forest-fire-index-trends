Subject. The text identified the combinations of weather, Fine Fuel Moisture Code and Drought Code under which large fires started on the Iberian Peninsula.

Establishes. On the Iberian Peninsula over 2001 to 2015, the study took the 814 fires larger than 500 ha from the Spanish and Portuguese fire databases (PDF p. 6). The data held only large fires, so no day without one entered the analysis (PDF p. 6). By Table 2 (PDF p. 10), those fires were 0.8 per cent of 100,335 fires and burned 1,485,890 of 2,918,492 ha, or 50.9 per cent. Of the four pyroregions of Trigo and others (2016), the Southwest held 373 large fires, the Northwest 279, the East 96 and the North 66 (Table 2, PDF p. 10). Weather came from the ERA-Interim reanalysis at noon standard time, on a 0.75 degree grid resampled to 0.11 degrees (PDF p. 7). Of the System, only the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and the Drought Code were analysed, with temperature, relative humidity and wind speed (PDF p. 7). The Duff Moisture Code was dropped, and the Initial Spread Index was judged redundant with the Fine Fuel Moisture Code (PDF p. 7). Neither the Buildup Index nor the Fire Weather Index itself entered the analysis (PDF pp. 7, 8). Rain was dropped as a variable but entered both codes (PDF p. 7). Temperature, relative humidity and both codes were averaged over the ignition day, the 7 days and the 30 days before the fire (Table 1, PDF p. 8). Wind speed entered for the ignition day only. A monthly temperature anomaly, the relative distance to the 85th percentile, was added (PDF p. 7, and Table 1, PDF p. 8). A multi-group principal component analysis across the four regions gave four large fire weather typologies, 'heat-driven', 'heat wave', 'seasonal drought' and 'wind-driven' (PDF pp. 2, 10, 11). By Table 3 (PDF p. 10), the four together explained 73.8 per cent of the variance in the North and 83.3 per cent in the Southwest. 'Heat-driven' alone explained 48.2 per cent in the East and 35.7 per cent in the Northwest, although the text on that page gave 35.7 per cent to the North. Under 'heat-driven', the text put both codes' loadings above 0.25, so fine and heavy fuels were dry together (PDF pp. 11, 14). Under 'seasonal drought', Figure 4 drew the Drought Code positive and the Fine Fuel Moisture Code negative at all three scales (PDF p. 11). The text read this as dry heavy fuels with moist fine fuels, high relative humidity and wind (PDF pp. 11, 14). Under 'heat wave', Figure 4 drew the Drought Code negative at all three scales while the Fine Fuel Moisture Code loading rose toward the ignition day (PDF p. 11). The text read this as fine fuels drying while heavy fuels stayed moist, although it called the Drought Code correlation positive (PDF pp. 11, 14). Clustering the scores gave six clusters, the largest holding 376 of the 814 fires under average conditions of all four typologies (PDF p. 12). Median fire size ran from 731 ha in cluster 4, 'heat-driven' fires under calm wind, to 1,020 ha in cluster 3, 'seasonal drought' (Table 4, PDF p. 12). The authors concluded that temperature, wind speed and the Drought Code at daily and monthly scales were the main factors driving large fires (PDF pp. 2, 16). After Cardil and others (2016), they judged one danger index hard to make representative over large heterogeneous areas (PDF p. 16). The caveat on aggregate indexes was stated in the abstract and Introduction as a reason for the design, and it was not tested (PDF pp. 2, 4).
