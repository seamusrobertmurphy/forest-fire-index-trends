Subject. The paper documented version 4 of the US National Fire Danger Rating System and compared it with version 2 at six sites over 2003 to 2017.

Establishes. The paper presented version 4 as the first revision of the US National Fire Danger Rating System since version 3 of 1988 (PDF pp. 1, 2). Its Table 1 dated version 4 to 2024 and gave the paper itself as the reference (PDF p. 2). The text stated that the update began in 2016 and that the final version was accepted for operational, nationwide use in March 2023 (PDF p. 14). Feedback came from the models' integration into the Weather Information Management System from 2017 and from field users over the 2023 fire season (PDF pp. 14, 15). Version 4 replaced the dead fuel moisture models with the Nelson-Carlson model, driven by hourly temperature, relative humidity, precipitation and solar radiation (PDF p. 4). It replaced the live fuel moisture model with one built on the Growing Season Index (PDF pp. 4 to 7). It cut the 20 fuel models of version 2 to five, grass (V), grass-shrub (W), brush (X), timber (Y) and slash (Z) (PDF pp. 7, 10, 11). Across 50,000 simulated moisture scenarios, the Energy Release Component of 18 of the 20 old fuel models correlated above 0.85 with another's, and no new pair did (PDF p. 10). The four danger metrics, the Energy Release Component, Spread Component, Burning Index and Ignition Component, were kept (PDF pp. 8, 12). Over 346 field measurements of six species, the new live fuel model at default settings had a coefficient of determination of 0.629, against 0.076 for version 2 (PDF p. 10). The version comparison used six sites on National Forest System lands, the Okanogan-Wenatchee, Lolo, Angeles, Prescott and Apalachicola National Forests and the Little Missouri National Grassland (PDF p. 8). Hourly weather from Remote Automated Weather Stations and fire records from the Fire Program Analysis Fire Occurrence Database covered every day of 2003 to 2017 (PDF p. 8). Both versions were computed once a day at the standard observation hour, typically 12:00 or 13:00 local time, and averaged over each site's stations (PDF p. 8). Version 2 ran fuel model G only, and version 4 ran all five new fuel models (PDF p. 8). Every daily series was converted to percentiles of its own 2003 to 2017 record, partly because the two versions rated danger on different absolute scales (PDF p. 8). Seasonality was compared on the Energy Release Component of fuel model G against that of fuel model Y, as 15-year monthly mean percentiles (PDF p. 9). Over the 72 site-months, those means had a coefficient of determination of 0.94 between versions (PDF p. 10, Fig. 8b on PDF p. 13). The abstract's correlation of 0.97 matched it, since 0.97 squared gave 0.94 (PDF p. 1). Skill was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve for telling fire days from days without fire (PDF p. 10). A fire day had at least one new fire reported in the site. A large fire day had one that grew beyond the site's 97th percentile of final fire size (PDF p. 8). Of 24 comparisons, six sites by four danger metrics with fuel model G against fuel model Y, 14 did not differ significantly (PDF p. 11). Version 4 scored higher in 8 and lower in 2, so the abstract's 92 per cent was 22 of 24 (PDF pp. 1, 11). The paper did not name the test that judged two areas significantly different. In the Lolo National Forest, the Energy Release Component scored 0.78 under version 2 and 0.85 under version 4 (PDF pp. 10, 11). Over 5,479 days, fire days ran from 213 in the Little Missouri National Grassland to 1,936 in the Angeles National Forest (Table 13, PDF p. 14). The Okanogan-Wenatchee National Forest had 1,081 fire days and 76 large fire days, the large fires exceeding 110 ha (Table 13, PDF p. 14). Its best fire day score, 0.853, came from the Energy Release Component of fuel model Y (Table 13, PDF p. 14). The three contrasts were fire days against days without fire, large fire days against days without fire, and large fire days against fire days (PDF p. 10). The best score for each site and contrast ran from 0.647 to 0.915 (Table 13, PDF p. 14). Field users judged version 4 as good as or better than version 2, though fuel model Y sometimes over-rated danger in hot, dry spells (PDF p. 15). The paper tested no trend, and it compared the versions' values only as percentiles, never on a common absolute scale.

Manuscript sentences checked

1. qmd L2196, "The United States issued version 4 of its National Fire Danger Rating System in 2024, with new fuel moisture models and the fuel models consolidated to five types [@jolly2024nfdrs]." Verdict, does not hold. Evidence, the text put the issue in March 2023, "Our updates to the US National Fire Danger Rating System started in 2016 and the final model version was accepted for operational, nationwide use in March 2023." (PDF p. 14, printed p. 14). Field users gave feedback "over the 2023 fire season" (PDF p. 15, printed p. 15). The only 2024 date given for version 4 was in Table 1, which listed this paper as its reference (PDF p. 2, printed p. 2). The rest of the sentence held. The abstract stated "Here we implement new fuel moisture models" and "We also consolidated fuel models to five fuel types" (PDF p. 1). The new models were the Nelson-Carlson dead fuel model and a live fuel model built on the Growing Season Index (PDF p. 4, printed p. 4). The five types were grass, grass-shrub, brush, timber and slash (PDF p. 10, printed p. 10, and Table 11, PDF p. 11). The citation also gave no page. Proposed wording, "In March 2023, the United States accepted version 4 of its National Fire Danger Rating System for operational, nationwide use [@jolly2024nfdrs, p. 14]. It replaced the dead and live fuel moisture models and consolidated the fuel models to five types [@jolly2024nfdrs, pp. 1, 4]."

2. qmd L2196, "Its index seasonality against the old version "across six US National Forests was very similar" and "V4 was as good or better than V2 at predicting fire days in 92% of the cases tested" [@jolly2024nfdrs, abstract]." Verdict, holds but the citation should give a page. Evidence, both quoted passages stood verbatim in the abstract, "Index seasonality compared between old (V2) and new USNFDRS models (v4) across six US National Forests was very similar" and "V4 was as good or better than V2 at predicting fire days in 92% of the cases tested" (PDF p. 1). PDF p. 1 printed no page number, and the next page printed 2, so the abstract was on p. 1. The sentence left out two facts that would change how a reader took it. First, the seasonality was compared on percentiles, in part because "Version 2 and Version 4 produce fire danger ratings on different absolute scales" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8). The paper concluded "Both versions demonstrated similar seasonality despite differences in absolute index values." (PDF p. 16, printed p. 16). The close agreement therefore said nothing about levels, where the manuscript found its version effect. Second, the 92 per cent was 22 of 24 fire day comparisons, six sites by four danger metrics (PDF p. 11, printed p. 11). Of the 24, 14 "were not distinguishable", 8 favoured version 4 and 2 favoured version 2 (PDF p. 11, printed p. 11). The quoted "six US National Forests" included the Little Missouri National Grassland (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8), but the words were the paper's own. Proposed wording, "Its index seasonality against the old version "across six US National Forests was very similar" [@jolly2024nfdrs, p. 1]. Over 2003 to 2017, the two versions were compared as percentiles, in part because "Version 2 and Version 4 produce fire danger ratings on different absolute scales" [@jolly2024nfdrs, p. 8]. Version 4 "was as good or better than V2 at predicting fire days in 92% of the cases tested" [@jolly2024nfdrs, p. 1]. Those cases were 24 comparisons of how well each version separated fire days from days without fire, and 14 of them did not differ significantly [@jolly2024nfdrs, p. 11]."

Bears on

- Introduction, "Two other national danger rating systems were replaced in the same decade." (qmd L2196). Adds to it, since the paper named the US, Canada and Australia as modernising their systems independently (PDF p. 2) and version 4, like FWI2025, took hourly weather inputs (Table 2, PDF p. 3).
- Introduction, "Neither compared the trend of one index under two versions of its own calculation." (qmd L2192). Adds to it, since the nearest version comparison, this one, also tested no trend and compared only percentile seasonality and fire day skill over 2003 to 2017 (PDF pp. 8 to 11).
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence." (qmd L2198). Adds to it, since the paper judged version 4 by "whether the updates resulted in any loss of predictive capability" against fire days (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8).
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1 nonetheless claimed continuity" (qmd L2200). Qualifies it by contrast, since the US developers stated outright that the two versions "produce fire danger ratings on different absolute scales" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8).
- Introduction, "FWI1987 on its 13:00 observation against FWI2025 on hourly weather would confound" (qmd L2200). Adds to it, since the US comparison was of that kind, with version 2 on daily summaries and version 4 on hourly weather with solar radiation (PDF pp. 3, 4, 8).
- Discussion, "The small difference in ignition skill between series was consistent with comparisons" (qmd L3572). Adds to it, since 14 of the 24 US fire day comparisons between versions did not differ significantly, and version 4 scored lower in 2 (PDF p. 11, printed p. 11).
- Discussion, "A series of percentile exceedances was unaffected by the version change" (qmd L3576). Adds to it, since the US developers held that, as the indices "are most commonly applied as percentiles", "their absolute magnitudes are less relevant" (PDF p. 7, printed p. 7).
- Discussion, "Fixed classes were already regional and tied to the period that calibrated them." (qmd L3576). Adds to it, since the paper said outputs "must be translated into critical breakpoints" in locally made plans, though it never said version 2 breakpoints had to be redrawn for version 4 (PDF p. 16).
- Discussion, "A percentile trend was portable across the version change only when its reference period was stated and kept fixed" (qmd L3576). Qualifies it, since the US percentiles took each version's whole 2003 to 2017 record as the base and so tested agreement in rank, not trend (PDF p. 8).
- Discussion, "The study covered only the Canadian part of one basin" (qmd L3619). Adds context only, since one US site, the Okanogan-Wenatchee National Forest, lay at the Canadian border in northern Washington on the Fig. 7 inset (PDF p. 13).
