Subject. The paper asked how the trend, spatial pattern and Fire Weather Index association of fires in mainland Portugal over 2001 to 2024 changed with final fire size.

Establishes. Over 2001 to 2024, the paper used 489,226 fire records with a valid final burned area from SGIF, the national fire information system of mainland Portugal (p. 3). Of these, 81.17 per cent burned less than 1 ha and 0.097 per cent reached 1000 ha or more. Fires of 1000 ha or more burned 1.761 of the 3.209 million ha burned, 54.87 per cent (p. 5). Fires of 100 ha or more were 0.678 per cent of records and 80.98 per cent of the area burned (p. 10). Annual counts declined significantly in all fires and in every class below 1000 ha (p. 5). For all fires, the decline was [Mann-Kendall τ = −0.703, p < 0.001], with a Sen slope of −1077.4 fires per year. Fires of 1000 ha or more showed no detectable trend [τ = −0.192, p = 0.196]. The trend method named a tie correction and no correction for serial correlation or for multiple testing across its six tests (p. 4). The authors did not compute the Fire Weather Index. They used the value stored with each record, produced operationally by the Portuguese Institute for Sea and Atmosphere and not recalculated (p. 3). The paper stated no version, observation hour or weather source for those values. Its only reference for the System itself was Van Wagner (1987) (pp. 2, 14). Its reference for the operational values, IPMA (2025), was the institute's 1991 to 2020 climate normals page (pp. 12, 13). The authors wrote that the procedure assigning a daily value to each fire could not be reconstructed (p. 12). Every record predated the 2025 release of FWI2025. Values produced at the time of each fire therefore came from an earlier version, but the paper did not say when they were produced. For the 488,590 fires with a valid index, binomial logistic regressions modelled whether final area reached 10, 100, 250, 500 or 1000 ha (p. 5). Each had the index per 10 units, calendar year as a continuous term and sine and cosine terms of month, with standard errors clustered by ignition date. A natural cubic spline of the index with four degrees of freedom improved fit at every threshold [likelihood ratio p < 0.001] (pp. 5, 8). Skill was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and the Brier score (p. 5). The index's contribution was the gain in AUC over a baseline of year and month terms without it. Adding the index raised AUC from 0.538 to 0.658 at 10 ha and from 0.717 to 0.884 at 1000 ha (Table 4, p. 10). The gain rose in point estimates from 0.120 at 10 ha to 0.166 at 1000 ha. Read by eye from Figure 6B (p. 11), the paired bootstrap intervals of neighbouring thresholds overlapped. The 1000 ha interval ran from about 0.13 to 0.21, and no test of the difference between thresholds was reported. Per 10 units of the index, the odds ratio was 1.56 at 10 ha and 2.58 at 1000 ha (p. 8). Their 95 per cent confidence intervals were 1.53 to 1.59 and 2.38 to 2.80. The median index on the ignition day was 20.2 for fires under 1 ha and 41.6 for fires of 1000 ha or more (p. 8). Their interquartile ranges were 11.1 to 29.6 and 33.5 to 50.0. The classes differed [Kruskal-Wallis H = 4288.2, p < 0.001], with a small effect size, ε² = 0.0088 (p. 8). Among 3312 fires of 100 ha or more, 474 (14.31 per cent) reached 1000 ha. For them, the index raised AUC only from 0.593 to 0.694, with an odds ratio of 1.64 (95 per cent interval 1.49 to 1.81) per 10 units (p. 9). Every model held one index effect over the whole of 2001 to 2024, beside an additive year term (eq. 7, p. 5). No interaction of the index with time was fitted, so whether its skill changed within the period was not tested. The only check of the index over time refitted the spline models to 2001 to 2020 and assessed them on 32,188 fires of 2021 to 2024 (pp. 5, 11). Holdout AUC with the index ran from 0.592 at 10 ha to 0.912 at 1000 ha (Table 5, p. 11). Its gain over the baseline was 0.131 at 10 ha, 0.121 at 100 ha and 0.213 to 0.263 from 250 ha up. By subtraction in Table 5, the baseline's holdout AUC was 0.461 at 10 ha, against 0.538 over the full period. At 10 ha, the holdout AUC with the index, 0.592, was also below its full-period value of 0.658, on a different sample. The larger holdout gain there came from a weaker baseline, not a stronger index. Calibration intercept and slope were −1.34 and 0.50 at 10 ha and −1.02 and 0.75 at 100 ha, and the slopes were 0.95 to 1.06 from 250 ha up. The authors read the holdout as support for ranking more than for calibrated probabilities, and treated the 500 and 1000 ha results, on 71 and 48 exceedances, with caution (pp. 9, 12). The paper's keyword, spatial non-stationarity, referred to its geographically weighted regressions of municipal structure, not to change over time (p. 5). Its outcome throughout was the final size of fires that had already started, never whether a fire started.

Bears on

- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence." (qmd L2198). Qualifies it. Nunes and Guerra judged the index on the final size of fires that had already started. The paper was not a test of occurrence and should not be cited as one.
- Introduction, "All of them predated the 2025 update, so every fire danger index among them was computed under FWI1987." (qmd L2190). Qualifies it if the paper is cited anywhere. Published in October 2026, it stated no version and cited only Van Wagner (1987), so it should be described as using the operational index, version unstated.
- Methods, "The season factor gave each season its own intercept, so the danger coefficient was identified only from day-to-day variation inside a season." (qmd L2712). Adds to it. The paper likewise measured the index against a baseline of year and month terms without it (p. 5), a published precedent for reporting skill net of seasonal timing.
- Methods, "Stationarity was tested by an interaction between the danger term and season number." (qmd L2712). Adds to it. The Portuguese models held the index effect fixed across 2001 to 2024 beside an additive year term, and checked time only by a 2021 to 2024 holdout (p. 5).
- Methods, "The same source warned that such thresholds "will be different in other regions"" (qmd L2572). Adds to it. In Portugal, half of the fires of 1000 ha or more started on days with an index above 41.6, against the extreme class of 30 used here (p. 8). Half of the fires under 1 ha started above 20.2.
- Discussion, "The small difference in ignition skill between series was consistent with comparisons of danger indices against fire records elsewhere." (qmd L3572). Qualifies it. The paper compared no indices and no versions, so it cannot be cited for this sentence, only for the area-burned caveat later in the paragraph.
- Discussion, "The same study found that "relying solely on fire-weather indices is insufficient"" (qmd L3572). Adds to it. Nunes and Guerra reached the same judgement for final fire size, supporting the index "but not as a stand-alone predictor of final extent" (p. 12). It is not proposed, because it repeats the point.
- Discussion, "The models here included the seasonal effect, and a model of one basin mean had no spatial effect to include." (qmd L3572). Adds to it. The Portuguese event models had year and month terms but no regional effects, and the authors judged that "some residual spatial confounding may remain" (p. 12).
- Discussion, "Fixed classes were already regional and tied to the period that calibrated them." (qmd L3576). Adds to it. Besides Possible addition 2, the paper reported that Fernandes (2019) found Portuguese index thresholds that rose with fire size and varied by region (p. 2). That is the paper's paraphrase, and Fernandes was not read here.
