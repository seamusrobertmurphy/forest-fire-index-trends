Subject. The paper estimated how much human-induced climate change raised the likelihood of the extreme fire weather and area burned of British Columbia's 2017 wildfire season.

Establishes. The paper ran an event attribution of the 2017 wildfire season in British Columbia, when a record 1.2 million ha burned and 65,000 people were displaced (printed p. 2). That area was 40 per cent above the previous record, set in 1958, in observations that began in 1950 (printed p. 2). The analysis covered the Southern Cordillera homogeneous fire regime zone within British Columbia, which held most of the 2017 fire activity, though the article never named the Okanagan (printed p. 3). There, the 2017 area burned was an order of magnitude above the previous record, while the count of fires above 4 ha was near average (printed p. 3). Hot and dry conditions in July and August followed a wet spring with fewer fires than normal through June (printed p. 3). The model was a 50-member ensemble of the CanRCM4 regional climate model on a 50 km grid, run over 1961 to 2020 (printed p. 3). Each member was driven by a member of the CanESM2 global model under historical natural and anthropogenic forcing to 2005 and the RCP8.5 scenario after it (printed p. 3). The ensemble was bias corrected toward the MERRA2 reanalysis by the multivariate method of Cannon (2018) (printed p. 3). Its fire weather indices used local noon values of MERRA2 from the Global Fire Weather Database, with MSWEP precipitation (printed pp. 3, 4). The decade 2011 to 2020 stood for the current climate and 1961 to 1970 for a climate with reduced human influence (printed p. 4). Each decade pooled 500 values, ten years from each of 50 members, fitted by a Gaussian kernel density estimator (printed p. 4). Change was measured by the fraction of attributable risk, the share of the current decade's probability in excess of the earlier decade's (printed p. 4). The probability ratio divided the current probability by the earlier one, and 1,000 resamples of each pool gave 90 per cent confidence intervals (printed pp. 4, 5). In gridded station observations for the BC Southern Cordillera, July and August 2017 ranked first since 1961 for both heat and dryness (printed p. 4). Human influence contributed about 96 per cent of the probability of the 2017 maximum temperature anomaly and raised its likelihood over 20 times (printed p. 4). Mean July and August precipitation anomalies showed no discernible trend over 1961 to 2020, so no human contribution to the 2017 dryness was identified (printed p. 6). Fire danger was measured as the regional mean of each season's 95th percentile of daily July and August values, against thresholds from the 2017 reanalysis (printed p. 6). The nine indices in Figure 3 were labelled FWI, FFMC, DMC, DC, ISI, BUI, DSR, ROS and HFI, defined in Table S1, which was not read (printed p. 6). For all of them, human influence accounted for 60 to 90 per cent of the risk of exceeding the 2017 value (printed p. 6). It raised the likelihood of such values by a factor of 2 to 4 (printed p. 6). Read by eye on the log axis of Figure 3b, the Drought Code's probability ratio lay between 5 and 6, above the range the text gave (printed p. 6). For the longest annual run of days above the July and August 90th percentile, human influence accounted for 55 to 95 per cent of the risk (printed p. 6). Figure 4 showed this for eight indices, without the Drought Code (printed p. 7). CanRCM4 did not simulate fire, so area burned came from a regression on climate and fire weather indices, with July mean temperature the best predictor (printed p. 6). Under leave-one-out cross-validation, it explained about 55 per cent of the variance of the natural log of annual area burned (printed pp. 6, 8). The 2017 area burned was about the 99th percentile of the current decade, and the same percentile was 7.1 to 11.0 times smaller in 1961 to 1970 (printed p. 7). The paper's alternative reading was that 86 to 91 per cent of the 2017 area burned could be attributed to anthropogenic climate change (printed p. 7). A province-wide repeat gave 84 to 87 per cent, with less confidence (printed p. 8). The paper stated that this result depended on the regression being realistic (printed p. 7). The regression did not account for forest management or human ignitions, and the analysis left out repeated suppression and the pine beetle infestation (printed p. 7). The 2018 season burned over 1.35 million ha, mostly in the north of the province, so the 2017 attributions did not apply to it directly (printed p. 8). The paper did not define detection or set it apart from attribution, and it used the word "detected" only for earlier findings of a human influence (printed pp. 2, 8). Its supporting information, which held the index definitions and Figures S1 to S10, was not in the reading folder.

Manuscript sentences checked

1. qmd L3619, "The study detected change and did not attribute it, because attribution required a comparison against a world without anthropogenic forcing, the human contribution to climate change [@kirchmeieryoung2019attribution]." Verdict, holds but the citation should give a page. Evidence, the paper's section 2.2 opened with "Event attribution compares the probability of occurrence of an event between a scenario including anthropogenic factors and a scenario without the anthropogenic inﬂuence." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 4). The paper's own term was "a scenario without the anthropogenic inﬂuence" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 4). Its plain language summary put the same idea as "an estimate of what the likelihood might have been without the human component" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 2). The words counterfactual and world did not appear anywhere in the article's text. Three points the sentence leaves out do not change its sense, but a referee who knows the paper may raise them. First, the definition was of event attribution, the attribution of one event, here the 2017 season. Second, the reference probability did not come from runs without human influence. It came from 1961 to 1970 of the all-forcing regional ensemble, used "to represent an alternative climate with reduced inﬂuence of human emissions" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 4). The paper supported that choice with natural-only runs of the driving global model, CanESM2. Their difference from all-forcing runs in 2011 to 2020 was similar to the temperature difference between the two decades (Figure S2, not read). Because other external influences were small over the period, the paper attributed the change between decades to "primarily anthropogenic forcing" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 4). Third, both probabilities were estimated from a 50-member regional climate model ensemble, which this study did not have. The paper's reference decade also preceded this study's record, which began in 1991. The paper did not itself set detection apart from attribution. It used "detected" only for earlier findings of a human influence, "such as has been detected in Western Canada" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 2), and "Wan et al. (2018) have detected an anthropogenic inﬂuence in summer (June–August) temperature over BC and the neighboring Yukon." (PDF p. 7, printed p. 8). The nearest it came was for precipitation, where it found "no discernible trend" (PDF p. 5, printed p. 6) and so did not "identify an anthropogenic contribution" (PDF p. 5, printed p. 6). The sentence cites the paper only for what attribution required, which the definition supports. Proposed wording, adding the page only, "The study detected change and did not attribute it, because attribution required a comparison against a world without anthropogenic forcing, the human contribution to climate change [@kirchmeieryoung2019attribution, p. 4]." Proposed wording in the paper's own term, "The study detected change and did not attribute it, because attribution required a comparison with a scenario without anthropogenic influence [@kirchmeieryoung2019attribution, p. 4]."

Bears on

- Introduction, "Four of the province's most severe seasons" (qmd L2190). Adds to it. In British Columbia, the paper recorded 1.2 million ha burned in 2017, 40 per cent above the 1958 record, and over 1.35 million ha in 2018 (printed pp. 2, 8).
- Introduction, "All of them predated the 2025 update" (qmd L2190). Adds to it. The paper computed its indices daily from local noon values and named Van Wagner (1987) among its processing sources, consistent with FWI1987 (printed pp. 3, 4).
- Introduction, table of expectations, "Mean Fire Weather Index and seasonal severity rating" (qmd L2209). Adds to it. In the BC Southern Cordillera, model runs made exceeding the 2017 value of the FWI's and DSR's 95th percentile 2 to 4 times as likely (printed p. 6).
- Introduction, table of expectations, "Days above each series' own 90th and 95th percentiles" (qmd L2211). Adds to it. Model runs for the BC Southern Cordillera attributed 55 to 95 per cent of the risk of long runs of days above the 90th percentile to human influence (printed p. 6).
- Introduction, table of expectations, "Mean Duff Moisture Code and peak Drought Code" (qmd L2213). Adds to it. In the BC Southern Cordillera, July and August maximum temperature rose strongly while precipitation showed no discernible trend, and Drought Code extremes became more likely (printed pp. 4, 6).
- Methods, "Fire history in the dry forests of the southern interior" (qmd L2448). Adds to it. The paper described the BC Southern Cordillera as "generally characterized by a mixed-severity ﬁre regime" (PDF p. 2, printed p. 3), citing Heyerdahl et al. (2012) and Marcoux et al. (2015).
- Discussion, "The ignition counts tested here therefore could not show" (qmd L3572). Adds to it. In 2017, the BC Southern Cordillera burned an order of magnitude more area than its previous record while its count of fires above 4 ha stayed near average (printed p. 3).
- Discussion, "No index of the El Niño Southern Oscillation" (qmd L3619). Adds to it. The paper relied on a 50-member ensemble for "a robust sampling of internal climate variability" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 4), which one observed record cannot give, and it named neither oscillation.
- Discussion, "Fuel typing did not vary through time" (qmd L3619). Adds to it. The paper's area burned regression likewise assumed stationary nonclimatic variability, and the analysis left out forest management, suppression and the pine beetle infestation (printed p. 7).
