Subject. The paper projected the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and the Daily Severity Rating across the Canadian boreal forests from 1976 to 2100. It used bias-corrected climate model output and checked it against values computed from station weather for 1976 to 2005.

Establishes. The sole author, of the Canadian Forest Service, drew daily station records for 1976 to 2014 from the Environment and Climate Change Canada climate database. They gave daily precipitation, maximum and minimum temperature, mean relative humidity and mean wind speed. On 23 August 1987, 185 stations reported precipitation and temperature and 138 reported all four, though the station maps themselves were labelled 1976-08-23. The station values were interpolated by thin plate smoothing splines to 30 km pixels, on easting and northing alone with no covariate, so elevation did not enter. Four Earth system models supplied one run each for 1976 to 2005 and for 2006 to 2100 under RCP 2.6, 4.5 and 8.5. The simulations were bias-corrected by quantile delta mapping and delta-downscaled to the same surface, and the four models were combined as a multi-model mean. Only two indices were reported, the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and the Daily Severity Rating, computed by the procedures of Van Wagner and Pickett (1985). Noon temperature was estimated as the minimum plus 0.78 times the daily range, and relative humidity and wind speed entered as daily means without adjustment. The value 0.78 appeared to rest on temperatures for the 10th to 20th days of May to September, 1971 to 2000, at six stations, Kamloops among them. The sentence that gave those stations named the indices, not the coefficient, as its subject. Trends in annual May to September means in each pixel were estimated over 1976 to 2100 by a Butterworth low-pass filter and the Theil-Sen estimator. A pixel's trend counted as significant when Spearman's rho and Kendall's tau both rejected no correlation with year at p < 0.05. Walker's test and the false discovery rate test then judged field significance. No correction for serial correlation in those tests was stated, although the text gave serial correlation as its reason for setting aside linear regression. Every trend test ran on series built from simulated climate, and no test was applied to the observed series. For 1976 to 2005, the boreal mean Fine Fuel Moisture Code was 74.08 from observations and 74.09 from simulations, and both Daily Severity Rating means were 2.03. Observed and simulated five-year mean surfaces correlated at 0.91 to 0.97 for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and 0.83 to 0.92 for the Daily Severity Rating. Agreement was judged by these means and correlations, and no slope was compared. In Figure 5, read from the page image, the smoothed curves of the observed series rose after the mid-1990s while those of the simulated series stayed nearly level. On the smoothed curves, observed precipitation and wind speed declined over 1976 to 2014 while every simulation rose. The text attributed the precipitation decline to the droughts of 2001 to 2002. Under RCP 8.5, the Fine Fuel Moisture Code was projected to rise significantly over almost the entire boreal forest, most in the east. Under RCP 2.6, increases were limited to the Taiga Shield East, Boreal Shield East and Atlantic Maritime ecozones and a small part of the Montane Cordillera. The Daily Severity Rating was projected to rise most along the southwestern edge of the boreal forests under RCP 8.5. The Fine Fuel Moisture Code was projected to hold near 80 throughout in the Taiga Cordillera, Boreal Cordillera and Taiga Plains. The text analysed neither the Fire Weather Index itself nor any fire record, and gave no clock hour beyond an estimated noon temperature. Among its slips were a 1997 to 2005 baseline for a 10,950 day series on PDF p. 7 and 577 against 557 HadGEM2-ES locations.

Bears on

- Introduction, "The one provincial trend analysis of the Fire Weather Index fitted generalised extreme value distributions with linear time trends to monthly maxima at 861 stations over 1970 to 2018 [@hubnerova2020fire]." (qmd L2190). Does not bear. The text tested no trend in observed index values, in British Columbia or elsewhere. Its tests ran on series from simulated climate over 1976 to 2100, and it stated "I assessed FFMC and DSR trends based on simulated climate data using the BLP filter and Theil–Sen estimator" (PDF p. 13, printed p. 13). The observed values were only drawn with smoothed curves, for 1976 to 2014 by ecozone, and compared with the simulations by means for 1976 to 2005 (PDF pp. 12 and 17, printed pp. 12 and 17). The study area was national, ten terrestrial ecozones that included the Boreal Cordillera and Montane Cordillera of British Columbia. Its map appeared to leave unshaded the southern Montane Cordillera near the border, the area of the Okanagan basin (PDF p. 2, printed p. 2), though the figure was too coarse to confirm this. The Fire Weather Index itself was not analysed, only the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and the Daily Severity Rating.
- Introduction, "All of them predated the 2025 update, so every fire danger index among them was computed under FWI1987." (qmd L2190). Adds to it. Published in 2024, the text computed its indices "following the standard procedures reported by Van Wagner and Pickett (1985)" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8). That listing was the source of the equations in the manuscript's FWI1987 code (qmd chunk fwi1987-system, L983). The weather input was not the System's noon observation. Noon temperature was estimated as "minimum temperature + k × (maximum temperature–minimum temperature), where k = 0.78" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8). Relative humidity and wind speed entered as daily means, and the text said "Other climate variables are less predictable; thus, I made no adjustments." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8). The station data were daily Environment and Climate Change Canada records for 1976 to 2014, interpolated to 30 km pixels (PDF pp. 5 to 7, printed pp. 5 to 7). The simulated inputs were bias-corrected, delta-downscaled output of four Earth system models (PDF pp. 5 to 8, printed pp. 5 to 8). For this text, FWI1987 named the equations but not the hour or the source of the weather.
- Discussion, "The one measured effect of input timing on a slope was the 0.22 to 0.39 days per decade by which daily aggregates inflated a global extreme-day trend [@matteo2025challenges]." (qmd L3568). Does not bear. The text measured no effect of input timing, input source or interpolation on any slope. It compared indices from observed and simulated weather by means and surface correlations, never by slope. It reported "The means of the two FFMCs in the plot were 74.08 (observational data) and 74.09 (historical simulation data)" (PDF p. 12, printed p. 12). Its Figure 5, read from the page image, drew the smoothed observed curve rising after the mid-1990s and the simulated curve nearly level (PDF p. 12, printed p. 12). No slope or test was given for either. Neither the estimated noon temperature nor the spline interpolation was tested for its effect on a trend.
