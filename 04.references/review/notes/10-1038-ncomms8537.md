Question Q1, a gridded trend in fire weather season length that covers Canada, estimated by Theil-Sen slopes and Mann-Kendall tests under a four-step serial autocorrelation treatment with no multiple-testing correction, and Q2 at the margin, because the FWI was computed from six-hourly and three-hourly reanalysis fields reduced to daily extremes rather than a noon observation.

Establishes Jolly, Cochrane, Freeborn, Holden, Brown, Williamson and Bowman, Nature Communications 6, 7537, pages 1 to 11, computed the US Burning Index, the Canadian FWI and the McArthur Forest Fire Danger Index from three reanalyses, NCEP Reanalysis I and II at about 210 km and ERA-Interim at about 78 km, for 1979 to 2013, after reducing the six-hourly or three-hourly fields to daily maximum and minimum temperature and relative humidity, maximum wind and total precipitation, and defined fire weather season length as the number of days a year on which the normalised index exceeded half its range, averaged across the nine index and data combinations. Slopes were Theil-Sen estimates with Mann-Kendall significance under the four-step prewhitening of Yue and others (2002), with no correction for multiple tests across grid cells. Season length rose significantly over 25.3 per cent of the vegetated surface, 29.6 million square kilometres, an 18.7 per cent rise in the global mean, and the North American continental mean rose 0.1519 days per year (p < 0.01), but the boreal forest and tundra biome showed no significant trend globally or on either northern continent, and the season length metric correlated only weakly with Canadian boreal burned area, Spearman r of 0.30 and 0.324 at p < 0.1, against 0.679 for the United States. The paper tested no station series, no Canadian FWI components beyond the final index, and no change over time in the danger to fire relationship.
