Subject. Trends in wildfire activity and seasonal climate across British Columbia from 1919 to 2021, and the rise in area burned since the mid-2000s.

Establishes. Across British Columbia over 1919 to 2021, the study compared annual area burned and fire counts with seasonal climate (pp. 1, 9). Fire perimeters came from the Canadian National Fire Database for 1919 to 1984 and the National Burned Area Composite for 1985 to 2021 (p. 9). Fires under 20 ha were excluded as inconsistently reported, and burned areas were adjusted for unburned islands and perimeter errors (p. 9). Climate came from ClimateNA v7.31 at 50 km resolution (p. 8). The variables were spring and summer mean temperature, total precipitation and Hargreaves climatic moisture deficit, with annual frost-free days (p. 8). Spring ran from March to May and summer from June to August (p. 8). Breakpoints came from segmented regression, and each segment was tested by a modified Mann-Kendall test with the variance correction of Hamed and Rao (pp. 9, 11). In Fig. 2, a solid segment marked a significant trend under a one-sided Mann-Kendall test at p < 0.10 (p. 4). Annual area burned fell from 1919 to a breakpoint in 2008 and then rose, both segments significant (pp. 3, 4). The count of fires larger than 20 ha fell until the late 1940s, and its fitted trend after that was not significant, as read from Fig. 2j (p. 4). Annual means were 277.7 fires of 20 ha or more and 212,000 ha over 1919 to 1950, against 116.8 fires and 89,000 ha over 1951 to 2000 (p. 2). The moisture deficit fell through most of the century and turned upward at breakpoints in 2011 in spring and 1999 in summer (pp. 3, 4). Only the spring rise was drawn as significant (Fig. 2e, 2f, p. 4). Per degree of warming, precipitation rose 3.34 per cent in spring and 5.74 per cent in summer (p. 3). The paper compared these with a minimum of 15 per cent, which it attributed to Flannigan and others (p. 3). Since the early 20th century, the annual number of frost-free days rose by 26.7 (p. 3). The date by which 2 per cent of the year's area had burned came 27.1 days earlier (p. 3). Its fitted line in Fig. 2h fell from about day 123 to about day 96 (p. 4). Over 1919 to 2021, summer moisture deficit had significant Spearman correlations of 0.69 with annual area burned and 0.74 with the count of fires over 20 ha (Fig. 3, p. 5). Spring values were 0.38 and 0.37, and the test was a Spearman correlation modified for serial correlation (pp. 5, 8–9). For the Central zone, which on Fig. 1a covers the southern interior, the paper reported warming of 0.98 °C over 1970 to 2021 (pp. 2, 4). It also reported an abrupt decline in summer precipitation and a rise in summer moisture deficit, shown only in Supplementary Fig. 2 (p. 4). The paper named 2017, 2018, 2021 and 2023 among the province's most severe seasons (pp. 1, 2). Its figure for 2023 was 1.75 million hectares burned by 24 August, a provincial record for area burned (p. 2). That season lay outside the analysed record, and the paper was accepted on 25 August 2023 and published online on 5 September 2023 (p. 9). In Fig. 2i, the 2018, 2017 and 2021 bars were the three tallest of 1919 to 2021, read from the figure (p. 4). The study computed no fire danger index (p. 8).

Manuscript sentences checked

1. qmd L2190, "In British Columbia, mapped fire perimeters and annual climate over 1919 to 2021 showed fire activity rising from 2005 onward after a century-long decline [@parisien2023abrupt]." Verdict, holds but the citation should give a page. Evidence, the abstract gave the data as "mapped wildﬁre perimeters and annual climate data for the period of 1919–2021" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1). It gave the finding as "after a century-long decline, ﬁre activity increased from 2005 onwards" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1). The title dated the turn "since the mid-2000s" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1). The body dated it "around the year 2000" (PDF p. 2, printed p. 2) and put the breakpoint in area burned in 2008 (p. 3). The body used "century-long decrease" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 3) for area burned, which Fig. 2i drew as a significant fall from 1919 to 2008 (p. 4). The sentence leaves out that the fitted rise was in area burned. The fitted trend in the count of fires of 20 ha or more was not significant after the late 1940s (Fig. 2j, p. 4). In a study of ignitions, a reader could take fire activity to include fire numbers, which this paper did not report as rising. Proposed wording, "In British Columbia, mapped fire perimeters and annual climate over 1919 to 2021 showed fire activity rising from 2005 onward after a century-long decline [@parisien2023abrupt, p. 1]." To close the omission, add "The rise was in the area burned by fires of 20 ha or more, which turned upward at a breakpoint in 2008 [@parisien2023abrupt, p. 3]. The fitted trend in the number of such fires was not significant after the late 1940s [@parisien2023abrupt, p. 4]."

2. qmd L2190, "Four of the province's most severe seasons of the last century fell in 2017, 2018, 2021 and 2023 [@parisien2023abrupt]." Verdict, holds but the citation should give a page. Evidence, the abstract opened with "four of the most severe wildﬁre seasons of the last century occurred in the past 7 years" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1), naming "2017, 2018, 2021, and 2023" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1). Two omissions could matter to a referee. The body's first sentence gave a shorter span and a stronger rank, "its four most severe wildﬁre seasons (2017, 2018, 2021, and 2023) of the last half-century" (PDF p. 2, printed p. 2). The analysed record ended in 2021 (p. 9). For 2023, the paper gave only area burned to date, "1.75 Mha in BC (as of August 24)" (PDF p. 2, printed p. 2), which broke the provincial record. The paper was accepted on 25 August 2023 (p. 9). It gave no numerical ranking of seasons, and its example of severity was area burned (p. 2). Read from Fig. 2i, the 2018, 2017 and 2021 bars were the three tallest of 1919 to 2021 (p. 4). Proposed wording, "Four of the province's most severe seasons of the last century fell in 2017, 2018, 2021 and 2023 [@parisien2023abrupt, p. 1]." To show the basis for 2023, add "By 24 August 2023, that season had burned 1.75 million hectares, a provincial record for area burned [@parisien2023abrupt, p. 2]."

3. qmd L2209, table cell, "Rising moisture deficits after 2005 [@parisien2023abrupt]". Verdict, does not hold. Evidence, the variable was the seasonal "Hargreaves CMD" (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8), the climatic moisture deficit of spring, March to May, and summer, June to August. In spring, its trend turned upward at a "statistical breakpoint in 2011" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 3). In summer, it turned at a "breakpoint in 1999" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 3). Fig. 2 drew the spring rise as significant and the summer rise as not, under a one-sided Mann-Kendall test at p < 0.10 (Fig. 2e, 2f and caption, p. 4). In the paper, 2005 marked the end of the "previously reported wetting trend" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 3), citing Meyn and others (2013). The abstract tied the 2005 rise in fire activity to "a sharp reversal in the wetting trend of the 20th century" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1). The paper's own breakpoints for the deficit were nonetheless 2011 and 1999. The abstract's "moisture deﬁcits have increased due to rapid warming and increased evaporative demand" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1) gave a direction but no year. The paper also found the deficit of the Central zone and the province higher in the early 20th century than in recent decades, though inland stations were then few (p. 4). Proposed wording, "Rising climatic moisture deficit in British Columbia after breakpoints in 2011 in spring and 1999 in summer, significant in spring only [@parisien2023abrupt, pp. 3–4]". The test, a one-sided Mann-Kendall test at p < 0.10, can be named if the cell has room. For the Central zone, a basis nearer the basin, the paper reported "an increase in summer CMD" (PDF p. 4, printed p. 4). On Fig. 1a, that zone covered the southern interior, which holds the Okanagan (p. 2). Its timing sat in Supplementary Fig. 2, which was not part of this text.

Bears on

- Introduction, "In Canada over 1959 to 2015, area burned and the number of large fires rose significantly" (qmd L2190). Qualifies it. In British Columbia, the count of fires of 20 ha or more had no significant fitted trend after the late 1940s, while area burned rose after 2008 (pp. 3–4).
- Introduction, "Rapid warming raised moisture deficits even where precipitation remained high" (qmd L2190). Qualifies it. The sentence restated this paper's abstract without a citation, and it moved the abstract's "Even as precipitation levels remain high" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 1), a statement about time, to one about place. Proposed wording, "Rapid warming raised moisture deficits even as precipitation remained high [@parisien2023abrupt, p. 1]."
- Introduction, "The one provincial trend analysis of the Fire Weather Index" (qmd L2190). Adds to it. The paper cited a global analysis, its reference 24 by Jain and others (2022), for the finding that "the southern half of BC has experienced a signiﬁcant increase in extremes of ﬁre-conducive weather" (PDF p. 5, printed p. 5).
- Introduction, "All of them predated the 2025 update" (qmd L2190). Qualifies it. This paper used no fire danger index, only ClimateNA temperature, precipitation, moisture deficit and frost-free days (p. 8), so the FWI1987 clause did not apply to it.
- Introduction, expectations table, "Per degree of warming, precipitation had to rise about 10 per cent" (qmd L2213). Qualifies it. This paper cited Flannigan and others (2016), apparently the paper keyed flannigan2015fuelmoisture, for "a minimum increase of 15% in precipitation" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 3) per degree of warming, so the row's figures need checking.
- Introduction, expectations table, "Per degree of warming, precipitation had to rise about 10 per cent" (qmd L2213). Adds to it. British Columbia precipitation rose 3.34 and 5.74 per cent per degree of warming in spring and summer (p. 3), against the row's 10 and 5 per cent.
- Introduction, expectations table, "Daily lightning, person-caused and all-cause ignitions" (qmd L2214). Adds to it. Over 1919 to 2021, summer moisture deficit had significant Spearman correlations of 0.69 with annual area burned and 0.74 with the count of fires over 20 ha (Fig. 3, p. 5).
- Discussion, "No index of the El Niño Southern Oscillation or the Pacific Decadal Oscillation entered any model" (qmd L3619). Adds to it. The paper said ignition and spread followed day-to-day weather superimposed on oscillations including both, "making ﬁngerprinting the speciﬁc effects of climate change on ﬁre activity challenging" (PDF p. 5, printed p. 5).
- Discussion, "The fixed window from 1 May to 30 September could not register a season that lengthened at either end" (qmd L3619). Adds to it. Across British Columbia, the date by which 2 per cent of annual area had burned came 27.1 days earlier since the early 20th century, reaching about 6 April (p. 3, Fig. 2h p. 4).
