Question. Q1.

Establishes. Queen, Dean, Stone, Jain, Renwick, Melia and Imada (Communications Earth and Environment 6, 387, 2025) regressed May to October mean Fire Weather Index from ERA5 at 30 km over western North America, 1951 to 2021, on anthropogenic and natural response patterns from the d4PDF-G and CAM5 large ensembles by optimal fingerprinting, with trends shown as least squares slopes, and estimated the anthropogenic share of the observed linear increase at 103 per cent for 1951 to 2021 with a natural share of minus 33 per cent, 81 to 188 per cent for 1959 to 2014, and 16 to 88 per cent for 1984 to 2014, while noting that ERA5 Fire Weather Index fell into the 1980s before rising, so a linear trend depends on its endpoints. No station trend and no Mann Kendall test; ERA5 was validated against station Fire Weather Index in a supplement.

Quotations

"Trends are the least-square linear regression slope of the time series." (p. 3, Fig. 1 caption)

"Over the maximum shared time window between the models, 1959–2014, the relative contribution of anthropogenic forcings to the observed linear increases in May-October FWI across the region is between ~81 and 188% as estimated by both models." (p. 4)

"Over the 1951–2021 time window, d4PDF-G results indicate an anthropogenic contribution of 103% and a natural contribution of −33%." (p. 4)

"Finally, over the commonly used post-1984 period, results from both models for 1984–2014 estimate 16–88% anthropogenic and 11–26% natural contributions." (p. 4)

"This may be in part due to the decadal variability highlighted in Fig. 2, which shows ERA5 FWI values decreasing into the 1980s before increasing into the current decade, making a linear trend analysis sensitive to endpoint selection." (p. 2)
