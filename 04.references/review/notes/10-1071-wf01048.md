Question Q3, how lightning fire occurrence in southern British Columbia has been related to a fire danger index and with what model.

Establishes Wierzchowski, Heathcott and Flannigan related 1961 to 1994 lightning fire density zones in the central cordillera, which includes the Okanagan Highlands, to interpolated two week means of the Daily Severity Rating for 1989 to 1994 by non parametric tests and stepwise discriminant analysis, with no daily count model, no seasonal term and no validation beyond classification accuracy, which was 38 per cent in British Columbia. No change over time in the relationship was tested; the paper cites unpublished park reports finding no change in fire climate over the century. The copy read is the author upload held in the NRCan Open Science and Technology Repository, printed pages 41 to 51. International Journal of Wildland Fire 11(1), 41 to 51.

Quotations

"The period of study is 1961–1994, although the effects of lightning-caused fire on long-term fire history is discussed." (p. 41)

"From 1989 to 1994, there were over 545 000 lightning strikes in Alberta that produced 377 documented fires, whereas in British Columbia about 166 000 lightning strikes produced 3432 documented fires. In other words, lightning effectiveness in British Columbia is 1 fire for about every 50 strikes, while in Alberta there is about 1 fire for every 1400 strikes." (p. 45)

"Average DSR values for four 2-week periods of the 1989–1994 fire seasons (July and August) were used to build spatial Severity Rating maps using an interpolation technique." (pp. 47 to 48)

"The results of the discriminant analysis runs on the British Columbia dataset pointed to the elevation as the primary variable controlling fire occurrence patterns, followed by the density of lightning strikes, the distribution of the Severity Rating values for the 1–15 August and 16–31 August periods and vegetation composition (coniferous stands and the mixed forest class)." (p. 49)

"However, the low overall classification accuracy (38%) and the low classification accuracies for individual zones, combined with the low eigenvalue (0.24) of the first canonical function, point to the overall poor performance of the selected variables in separating the four fire density zones." (p. 49)
