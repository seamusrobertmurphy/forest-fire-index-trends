Subject. The text described how British Columbia's Ministry of Forests forecast fire intensity through the day from the mid 1990s, to keep firefighters safe.

Establishes. The paper came from a presentation at the 4th Fire and Forest Meteorology Conference, held in Reno, Nevada, from 13 to 15 November 2001. On 20 July 1994, the Garnet Fire started outside Penticton, and after the wind changed direction on 25 July, it spread rapidly. Over 3,000 people were evacuated, and 18 homes were lost in the Upper Carmi subdivision. In 1995, the Ministry introduced its Fire Weather and Behavior Advisory and Warning System, to address weaknesses found by the review of that fire. The paper called it the first system of its kind in Canada. By 2002, the Ministry produced daily curves of forecast fire intensity through the day, for potential and ongoing fires. A fire weather forecaster supplied weather for solar noon, from which daily codes and indices were forecast for approximately 40 weather stations for each of six regional fire centres. Each station was given one representative fuel type of the Fire Behavior Prediction System. Hourly values of the Fine Fuel Moisture Code came from the diurnal equations of Lawson, Armitage and Hoskins (1996), which worked mainly from the time of day. The forecast afternoon wind speed was applied to every hour. The equations needed one relative humidity between 06:00 and 11:59 local standard time for the morning hours, and the noon value was used for it. Spot forecasts for single incidents used hourly inputs, including mesoscale winds. A Fire Behavior Advisory was issued when forecast fire intensity for a fuel type exceeded Intensity Class 4, above 4,000 kilowatts per metre. The curves were first drawn by hand and faxed to fire crew bases. From May 2000, WeatherPro3 software drew them automatically for up to 3 days ahead and added the critical surface intensity for crowning. The paper reported no test of the forecasts against observed weather, fuel moisture or fire behaviour. A full reading and a search for accuracy, evaluation, validation, verification, error, bias, skill and comparison found none. The only comparison with actual conditions it named was operational, with incident commanders reporting actual weather and fire behaviour back to dispatch. It proposed more time-specific wind forecasts than the single noon wind. Options were the climatological wind trend of Beck and Trevitt (1989), or winds across the landscape from airflow and atmospheric models. The equations' drawback, it stated, arose on days whose actual temperature and humidity departed sharply from the assumed diurnal trend. For that, it proposed the method of Beck and Trevitt (1989), with the hourly Fine Fuel Moisture Code, at least in rainless periods. That method estimated hourly temperature, relative humidity and wind speed from forecast daily maxima and minima, latitude and calendar date. On variation through the day, it called the daily cycle of fire activity with weather, dead fuel moisture and fuel temperature a well established fact of fire management. It stated that the daily calculation was designed to represent peak danger near 16:00 local standard time, under a normal diurnal pattern. It also stated that fine fuel moisture changed most from hour to hour. Fig. 2 (printed p. 178) was a sample diagram for the Garnet Fire on 25 July 1994, in the ponderosa pine and Douglas-fir fuel type C-7. It used forecast indices for Penticton R/S, with a 13:00 wind of 15 kilometres per hour and relative humidity of 22 per cent. Its 17:00 Fine Fuel Moisture Code was 95 and its Buildup Index 152, with times in Pacific daylight time and the ground assumed level. Forecast head fire intensity rose from Class 3 in the early morning to a Class 5 peak of about 8,000 kilowatts per metre between 16:00 and 18:00. It fell back to Class 4 by midnight. Intensities above 4,000 kilowatts per metre were forecast from 11:00 to 21:00, and a wind shift from northwest to southwest after 13:00. The text did not say whether the diagram was drawn that day or later for training. Fig. 3 (printed p. 179) showed Sparwood, labelled 2707, in fuel type C-3, mature jack or lodgepole pine, over 28 to 31 August of an unstated year. Its head fire intensity peaked near 16:00 and was lowest near 05:00 each day, in a time zone not stated. The daily peaks rose from about 1,500 to about 11,000 kilowatts per metre, and only the last crossed the critical surface intensity for crowning. The curves also jumped between consecutive morning hours on some days, which the text did not explain.

Bears on

- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2188). Adds to it. The text named the four elements, dry-bulb temperature, relative humidity, 10 m open wind and 24 hour rain, read at 12:00 local standard time (printed p. 174). It cited Turner and Lawson (1978) for that standard.
- Introduction, "In 1987, the year Van Wagner specified FWI1987, he called its weather input" (qmd L2188). Qualifies it. Fifteen years later, the text judged the once daily calculation "usually adequate for most daily preparedness planning purposes" (printed p. 174). Fire potential, it added, often had to be predicted through the day.
- Introduction, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since" (qmd L2188). Adds to it. The text paired Van Wagner (1977) with Alexander, Lee and Lee (1984), whose title names hourly calculation of the Initial Spread Index and Fire Weather Index too (printed pp. 174, 180). It called the hourly routine "more data-demanding" than the time-of-day scheme.
- Introduction, "The diurnally adjusted code came from look-up tables" (qmd L2188). Qualifies it. The text attributed the code to Lawson, Armitage and Hoskins (1996), which estimated an hour's value "principally as a function of time of day" (printed p. 179). Those tables still needed one morning relative humidity, between 06:00 and 11:59 local standard time (printed p. 177).
- Methods, "The Fine Fuel Moisture Code followed the hourly method of" (qmd L2466). Adds to it. In 2002, the text proposed the same hourly code, with Alexander, Lee and Lee (1984), for days departing from "the assumed diurnal trend" of the time-of-day tables (printed p. 179).
- Methods, "Those extremes were expanded to an hourly stream by the report's modification" (qmd L2476). Adds to it. The text gave that method's inputs as forecast daily maxima and minima, "routinely provided by fire weather forecasters", with latitude and calendar date (printed p. 179). Its outputs were hourly temperature, relative humidity and wind speed. British Columbia's own daily forecasts applied the afternoon wind to every hour instead (printed p. 177).
- Methods, the sentence that gave Van Wagner's 16:00 peak (qmd L2569 by the project notes of 2026-11-06, not in the context file, so its words were not checked). Adds to it. The text stated that the daily calculation was "designed to represent the peak fire danger conditions at around 1600 hour LST or 1700 hour daylight saving time, assuming a normal diurnal pattern" (printed p. 174). Fig. 2 gave its daily codes at 17:00 Pacific daylight time, and the Fig. 3 curves peaked near 16:00 (printed pp. 178, 179).
- Methods, "Fire history in the dry forests of the southern interior was reconstructed from tree rings." (qmd L2448). Adds to it. The Garnet Fire, which started near Penticton on 20 July 1994, fell inside the study window and led to British Columbia's advisory system (printed p. 176). The Fig. 2 forecast for it used Penticton R/S weather, and the text did not say whether that station was the study's Penticton RS (printed p. 178).
- Discussion, "Fixed classes were already regional and tied to the period that calibrated them." (qmd L3576). Adds to it. By 2002, British Columbia's advisories were set off by forecast fire intensity above Intensity Class 4, 4,000 kilowatts per metre, for a named fuel type (printed p. 177). The text described those intensity classes as most commonly used across Canada, Alaska and New Zealand (printed p. 174).
