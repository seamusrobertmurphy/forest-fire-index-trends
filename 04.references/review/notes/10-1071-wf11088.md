Question Q3, how daily fire occurrence has been modelled against fire weather in British Columbia, with what distribution, what seasonal terms, what validation, and whether the danger to occurrence relationship varied over time.

Establishes Magnussen and Taylor modelled the daily probability of at least one fire in each of the 20 by 20 km cells of the National Forest Inventory grid over British Columbia for the fire seasons of 1981 to 2000, 1 April to 30 September, by twenty year-specific binary logistic regressions with piecewise linear spline terms, and modelled the number of fires on a fire day by a zero-truncated Poisson regression, not a negative binomial. No month, week or day term entered the models, by design, because the authors held that such terms could mask the physical drivers and that seasonal patterns in British Columbia were not stable. The models were fitted year by year because the regression coefficients varied between years with a coefficient of variation typically above 100 per cent, which the authors read as a random year effect, and that year to year change in the weather to occurrence relationship is the nearest published statement to a stationarity test for the province, although no formal test of a trend in the coefficients was made. Validation was leave one year out, with the mean of the remaining year-specific models as the prediction; daily correlation was 0.55 for human-caused fires in 1985 and 0.83 for lightning fires in south-eastern British Columbia in 1991 with strike counts as a predictor, and 0.36 for lightning fires without strike data in 1995. Day to day autocorrelation of the binary fire indicator was rejected in only a handful of cell years and spatial autocorrelation between neighbouring cells averaged 0.034. International Journal of Wildland Fire 21(4), 342 to 356.
