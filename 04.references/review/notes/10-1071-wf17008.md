Subject. A gridded trend analysis of fire season length, spread days and the annual 99th percentile of the Fire Weather Index, computed under FWI1987 from the North American Regional Reanalysis.

Establishes. The text computed the Fire Weather Index System of Van Wagner (1987) with the R package cffdrs, so every index was FWI1987. Its weather came from the North American Regional Reanalysis, 3-hourly on a 32 km grid, from 2 January 1979 to 31 December 2015. Temperature, relative humidity and wind speed were taken at 1200 hours local standard time, interpolated linearly where no 3-hourly step fell at that hour. The 24 hour precipitation was a rolling sum of eight consecutive 3-hourly totals. The text did not say how that window was aligned with 1200 hours. Spread days were the days per year with a Fire Weather Index above 19. The text linked them to days when a large share of area burned, citing Podur and Wotton (2011). FWI99 was the year's 99th percentile of the Fire Weather Index, a level in index units rather than a count of days. The text did not say whether that percentile was taken over all days of the year or over fire season days only. Trends were Theil-Sen slopes tested by the Mann-Kendall test at each grid cell. Field significance came from a bootstrap that permuted the years alike at every cell, with 1000 samples, and from false discovery rate control. That control was applied to a regional Mann-Kendall statistic over blocks of 3 by 3 cells, at α = 0.05 and 0.25. The main text applied no correction for serial correlation, judging its effect minimal on annual series. From January 2003, the reanalysis no longer assimilated Canadian precipitation observations, and trends over 1979 to 2015 showed a marked discontinuity at the Canada and United States border. Spread days and FWI99 were therefore tested over Canada for 1979 to 2002 only, and over the contiguous United States for 1979 to 2015. Mexico and Alaska were left out of the index calculations, so only fire season length, start and end covered North America for 1979 to 2015. Over Canada in 1979 to 2002, mean spread days rose from 16.4 to 19.8 days, a rate of 1.6 days per decade. Over the contiguous United States in 1979 to 2015, they rose from 95.0 to 104.4, a rate of 2.6 days per decade. Over Canada in 1979 to 2002, mean FWI99 rose from 26.1 to 27.0, a rate of 0.4 per decade. Over the contiguous United States in 1979 to 2015, it rose from 55.4 to 56.6, a rate of 0.3 per decade. At α = 0.05, Table 1 gave locally significant positive spread day trends of 9.5 per cent over Canada and 22.0 per cent over the contiguous United States. Both were field significant by the bootstrap, and after false discovery rate control the percentages were 1.3 and 12.2. For FWI99, the matching percentages were 10.2 and 21.9 locally, both field significant, and 5.0 and 13.9 after control (Table 1, PDF p. 9, printed p. 1017). Over Canada, negative spread day trends covered much of southern British Columbia, and negative FWI99 trends covered most of the province. After false discovery rate control at α = 0.05, the negative percentages over Canada were 0.3 for spread days and 1.5 for FWI99. The Conclusions confined the reduction in severe fire weather to a small part of British Columbia. In the same sentence, the clause "spread days increase" contradicted Table 1 and was likely a misprint for a decrease, so it was not relied on here. The only statement on how input timing affected a result concerned fire season length. Its thresholds were adjusted from 12 and 5 °C to 11 and 6 °C, because 3-hourly data could miss the daily maximum. The text reported little change in the calculated fire season length. It tested no effect of the noon interpolation, the 3-hourly sampling or daily aggregation on spread days or FWI99. It warned that 32 km areal averages might fail to resolve local topographic effects, particularly on wind speed. The text gave 21 621 land cells for North America, 9773 for Canada and 7857 for the contiguous United States. Table 1 gave 20993, 9069 and 7636 for the same regions.
