Subject. The paper set out the history of fire danger rating in Australia, the requirements for a new system and the design of the Australian Fire Danger Rating System.

Establishes. The paper was the first of five in a special section on the Australian Fire Danger Rating System. In Australia, fire danger was rated from hazard sticks in the 1930s, then from McArthur's Forest and Grassland Fire Danger Indices of the 1950s and 1960s. These were derived from experimental fires and later related to weather alone, so that ratings could be forecast. The danger scale was set from 1 to 100, where 100 stood for hypothetical worst-case summer conditions. After the Black Saturday fires of 2009 in Victoria, which burned over 450,000 ha and killed 173 people, a Catastrophic category was added above Extreme. The same revision moved the existing ratings to higher Fire Danger Index values. Research begun at the same time led to no change in the ratings, because the mechanics of the McArthur indices could not be adjusted to include new knowledge. Their fixed structure and the lack of clear definitions for their rating classes had also made performance hard to assess beyond general or anecdotal observation. A collaborative study set the requirements in 2015, development began in 2017, and the new system replaced the McArthur-based ratings throughout Australia in September 2022. Its research prototype computed forward rate of spread and fireline intensity from models for eight fuel types and hourly forecast weather on a 1.5 km grid. These gave a Fire Behaviour Index and up to six categories, with thresholds set by fuel type where fire behaviour changed the management strategy or the potential impacts. The authors judged fire behaviour calculations more suitable than fire frequency or index climatology as the basis of the system. Three Red Flag warnings marked wind change, long-distance spotting and atmospheric instability, the last where the daily maximum C-Haines index passed its 95th percentile climatological value. During the 2017 to 2018 southern fire season, 71 participants from fire and land management agencies filed 265 observation reports in a live trial of the prototype. Another 72 case study reports were added to cover fires of significant consequence. Each incident was given an observed rating by expert judgement, which was compared with the forecast rating. The prototype forecast fire danger accurately 56 per cent of the time, against 43 per cent for the McArthur system, and tended to overpredict rather than underpredict. The paper's account of the trial cited Grootemaat and others (2024). It noted that the system had since been modified to improve performance and accuracy. It reported no comparison of the new system's index values, seasonality or trends with those of the McArthur indices. Its Fig. 5, a Queensland screenshot, showed the daily maximum new rating beside the daily maximum Fire Danger Index, without analysis.

- Discussion, "Fixed classes were already regional and tied to the period that calibrated them." (qmd L3576). Adds to it. In 2009, Australia moved its ratings to higher Fire Danger Index values and added a Catastrophic class, and the new system set thresholds by fuel type.
