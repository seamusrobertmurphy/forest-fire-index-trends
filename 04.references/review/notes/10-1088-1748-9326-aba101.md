Subject. The letter projected fire size and annual area burned in Canada's forests to 2100 from the number of days on which fires spread.

Establishes. Across the forested area of Canada, the study used the 16 homogeneous fire regime zones of Boulanger and others (2014) as its units (printed p. 2). It mapped the daily growth of fires of 200 ha or more over 2001 to 2017, from National Fire Database perimeters and MODIS hotspots (printed pp. 2–3). Earlier fires were left out because MODIS data did not exist before 2001 (printed p. 3). A spread day, which the text also called a realized spread day, was a day on which the mapped spread distance exceeded 240 m (printed pp. 3–4). That distance matched a rate of spread of at least 1 m per minute over a four hour burning period with circular growth (printed p. 3). The one threshold held in all 16 zones, as recommended by Wang and others (2014, 2017) (printed p. 10). A threshold of 2 m per minute fitted slightly better nationally, but models could then not be fitted in the northeastern zones (printed p. 10). The authors judged that the best threshold might depend on the area, a question they left open (printed p. 10). A potential spread day was a day of fire conducive weather, used to model the count of realized spread days (printed p. 4). The text named no specific fire danger index, and it gave no threshold of the Fire Weather Index, the Initial Spread Index or any other index for it. Any such threshold would be in Wang and others (2014, 2017), which the letter cited for the method. Distributions of spread days by zone, for 1981 to 2010 and three periods to 2100, were taken from Wang and others (2017) (printed p. 4). The future distributions came from three climate models under three forcing scenarios (printed p. 4). The acknowledgements thanked the Northern Forestry Centre for interpolated fire weather data (printed p. 11). Fire size was fitted in each zone as log(y) = a + 2 log(x), a power law with its exponent fixed at two (printed p. 3). On Figure 2(B), the rate coefficient a ran across four classes from 4.86 to 6.60 (printed p. 5). The lowest class, 4.86 to 4.98, held SY, IC and SC in the west, and the highest held ES, EJB and NAT in the northeast. On Figure 2(C), the ratio of median realized to median potential spread days over 2001 to 2017 ran across four classes from 0.14 to 0.75 (printed p. 5). The lowest class, 0.14 to 0.25, held P, IC and SC, the three zones that covered southern British Columbia, with EJB and NAT in the east. The text placed the lower ratios mostly in the southern boreal and in the mountains of the coastal provinces (printed p. 5). It attributed the low southern ratios partly, and with a hedge, to suppression, and speculated that in the Rocky Mountain area fire growth changed with altitude (printed p. 9). Summarised over models and scenarios, mean fire size was projected to rise 20, 32 and 64 per cent by the 2020s, 2050s and 2080s (printed p. 5). The printed mean sizes were 3626 ha for the 2020s and 3618 ha for the 2050s, against 2747 ha at baseline. Both implied about 32 per cent, so the 20 per cent printed for the 2020s did not follow from them. Annual area burned rose 25, 45 and 93 per cent, from a median of 2.57 Mha to 3.21, 3.73 and 4.96 Mha (printed p. 6). For SC, fire size rose 85 per cent in the 2020s and 107 per cent in the 2050s, the largest of the 16 zones in both periods (printed p. 5). There, a baseline 99th percentile fire became about twice as frequent by 2100, the low end of a range reaching nine times in WJB, with only slight change in SY (printed p. 6). The main text held no table, and the tables it cited, S1 to S8, were in the online supplement, which was not supplied. The text gave no value for the Montane Cordillera or any other ecozone, and none for British Columbia by name. Figure 1 drew no provincial boundaries (printed p. 3). Read against the coastline, the 49th parallel and the scale bar, the Okanagan basin fell in SC, the Southern cordillera zone, west of the narrow IC strip. That reading was approximate, but IC and SC shared the lowest class in both Figure 2(B) and Figure 2(C), so it changed no value reported here. The manuscript reported from Wang and others (2025) a Fire Weather Index threshold estimated from area burned over 2001 to 2021, nationally and by ecozone. This letter estimated no index threshold, held one spread distance fixed in advance for all zones, and drew on fire records only to 2017. Whether Wang and others (2025) used the same perimeters and hotspots could not be checked from this text.

Bears on

- Methods, "The same source warned that such thresholds" (qmd L2572). Adds to it. The letter judged that "An optimal spread day threshold may be somewhat area dependent" (PDF p. 11, printed p. 10), though its threshold was a daily spread distance, not a Fire Weather Index.

- Discussion, "Fixed classes were already regional and tied to the period" (qmd L3576). Adds to it. Over 2001 to 2017, the share of fire conducive days realized as spread ranged by zone from 0.14 to 0.75, in the lowest class across southern British Columbia (printed p. 5).
- Discussion, "In Canada's forests over 2001 to 2021, the Fire Weather Index below which half the area burned" (qmd L3576). Qualifies any pairing with this letter. The letter set no Fire Weather Index threshold, and it used fire regime zones, not ecozones, with fire records only to 2017 (printed pp. 2–3, 10).
- Discussion, "The ignition counts tested here therefore could not show" (qmd L3572). Adds to it. The letter fitted fire size to the square of a fire's spread days (printed p. 3), so differences between series in days conducive to spread would not pass in proportion into area burned.
