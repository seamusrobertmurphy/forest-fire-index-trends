Subject. The letter tested trends in climate and fire activity over 1970 to 2019, and in reburning and burn severity from 1985, across Alberta's forest protection area. It then related the detrended series to each other by correlation and path analysis.

Establishes. The study area was 409 350 km² of boreal forest and foothills. It covered the 60 per cent of Alberta in the forest protection area and the part of Wood Buffalo National Park in the Northwest Territories. Climate came from ClimateNA, which downscaled 0.5 degree gridded variables to points on a 28 km by 52 km grid. Each value was a departure from the point's 1951 to 1980 normal, and the departures were averaged over all points for each year. The variables were spring and summer maximum temperature, relative humidity and vapour pressure deficit, and spring, summer and total annual precipitation. No Fire Weather Index System component was computed, and Van Wagner (1987) was cited once, for the choice of these variables. Fire activity came from the Canadian National Fire Database, as points for fires under 200 ha and polygons for larger fires. Fires under 2 ha were dropped, and the 1970 start, the 2 ha floor and the split by size were chosen to correct for changes in reporting practice. For each annual series, the authors fitted a Theil-Sen slope and ran a one-sided Mann-Kendall test at a significance level of 0.05. Where an autocorrelation function showed serial correlation, the modified test of Hamed and Rao (1998) replaced the ordinary one. Neither prewhitening nor a correction for multiple testing was described across the 31 trends in Tables 2 to 4. Every p-value was one-sided, and the text gave no time unit for its slopes. Over 1970 to 2019, summer maximum temperature rose (β = 0.029 °C, p < 0.001) and total annual precipitation fell (β = −0.725 mm, p = 0.039). Summer relative humidity fell (β = −0.054 per cent, p < 0.001) and summer vapour pressure deficit rose (β = 0.017 hPa, p < 0.001). Spring relative humidity fell (β = −0.018 per cent, p = 0.015). Spring maximum temperature, spring vapour pressure deficit and spring and summer precipitation showed no significant trend. Large fires of 200 ha or more rose in number (β = 0.300, p = 0.011) and as a share of all fires (β = 0.501, p < 0.001). On a log(ha) scale, annual area burned rose (β = 0.024, p < 0.001) and so did the 90th percentile fire size (β = 0.018, p < 0.001). The number of fires of 2 ha or more showed no trend (β = −0.026, p = 0.487), nor did median fire size (β = 0.003, p = 0.210). The share of area burned by large fires rose (β = 0.185, p = 0.001). Over 1985 to 2019, the number of reburns within 15 years rose (β = 0.278, p < 0.001). Area reburned, on a log(ha + 0.1) scale, rose too (β = 0.067, p = 0.002). Over 1985 to 2018, the share of unburned islands inside large fire perimeters fell (β = −0.358, p < 0.001). Median burn severity, as relativized burn ratio, rose in open conifer (β = 2.325, p = 0.026) and mixedwood forest (β = 1.630, p = 0.029). Dense conifer and broadleaf forest showed no significant trend. In the path analysis, total annual precipitation had a strong negative effect on the number of large fires, area burned and 90th percentile fire size. Summer vapour pressure deficit raised area burned and 90th percentile fire size, and spring vapour pressure deficit had no significant effect. The text did not split fires by cause and did not examine daily fire weather, and it named both as limits. The prose said spring dried by vapour pressure deficit, but Table 2 printed p = 0.235 for it. The prose also gave p = 0.001 for the share of large fires that reburned, where Table 3 printed p < 0.001.

Bears on

- Introduction, "The one provincial trend analysis of the Fire Weather Index fitted generalised extreme value distributions with linear time trends to monthly maxima at 861 stations over 1970 to 2018 [@hubnerova2020fire]." (qmd L2190). Does not bear. Whitman and others tested no trend in any Fire Weather Index System value, in Alberta or elsewhere. Their climate series were seasonal and annual departures from gridded data downscaled by ClimateNA, over 1970 to 2019, tested by Theil-Sen slopes and one-sided Mann-Kendall tests. The variables were "spring, summer and total annual precipitation (PPT, mm), spring and summer average maximum temperatures (Tmax, ◦ C), spring and summer mean relative humidity (RH, %), and spring and summer mean vapour pressure deficit (VPD, hPa; calculated from average temperature and RH)" (PDF p. 4, printed p. 3). The authors placed daily fire weather outside their scope. "It is plausible that if we had examined daily fire weather rather than annual climate it would have provided additional insight into wildfire dynamics occurring at finer timescales (e.g. minutes to hours), beyond the scope of this work [95]." (PDF p. 10, printed p. 9). The text analysed trends in climate and fire across most of a province, not in the index, so the sentence held under either reading of "provincial".
- Introduction, "All of them predated the 2025 update, so every fire danger index among them was computed under FWI1987." (qmd L2190). Does not bear. The text computed no fire danger index, so it had no observation time, no station record and no System equations to report. Its inputs were seasonal and annual climate values downscaled from a gridded source. "Gridded climate variables (0.5◦ × 0.5◦ ) were downscaled to a 28 km East-West × 52 km North-South grid of points with known elevations [41]." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 3). Van Wagner (1987), its reference [43], appeared once, beside Williams and others (2014), to support choosing climate variables "with known links to wildfire" (PDF p. 4, printed p. 3). The text, published in 2022, held no index, so adding it to the paragraph would leave the sentence true.
- Introduction, "In Canada over 1959 to 2015, area burned and the number of large fires rose significantly, and in most of western Canada lightning-caused fires rose "whereas human-caused fires are either stable or declining throughout the country" [@hanes2019regime, abstract]." (qmd L2190). Adds to it, for the first clause only. Over 1970 to 2019 in the Alberta study area, one-sided Mann-Kendall tests found the same rise in large fires and area burned. "The number of large (≥200 ha) fires and percentage of ignitions becoming large fires both significantly increased (β = 0.300, p = 0.011; β = 0.501, p < 0.001 respectively), indicating a shift towards the dominance of large fires." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 4). "Similarly, significant increases in AAB (β = 0.024, p < 0.001) paralleled the concurrent increases in the occurrence and size of large fires (table 3; figure 2(c))." (PDF p. 6, printed p. 5). Table 3 printed no trend in the number of fires of 2 ha or more, a slope of −0.026 with p = 0.487 (PDF p. 8, printed p. 7). The authors called their results "broadly consistent" (PDF p. 9, printed p. 8) with Hanes and others (2019), their reference [21]. The text did not split fires by cause, so nothing in it tested the lightning and human-caused clause. "This work did not account for some fire regime drivers, such as fuel accumulation over time due to fire suppression and land-use change [90, 91], or changing patterns of ignition sources (e.g. lightning) [92]." (PDF p. 10, printed p. 9). Its one cause statement was a fixed share from Tymstra and others (2005), half lightning and half human (PDF p. 3, printed p. 2).
- Methods, "Significance was assessed under the variance correction of @hamed1998mmk for serial correlation, without which the test rejected too often on trendless series [@yue2002autocorrelation; @bayazit2007prewhiten]." (qmd L2702). Adds to it. Whitman and others paired Theil-Sen slopes with the Mann-Kendall test and switched to the Hamed and Rao (1998) test, their reference [52], for serially correlated series. "If an autocorrelation function indicated the time series data were temporally (serially) autocorrelated, we instead used a modified Mann-Kendall trend test for serially correlated data [52] from the modifiedmk package [53]." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 4). Their practice differed in two ways, since the tests were one-sided and the correction ran only on series that the autocorrelation function flagged. The text was a Canadian fire regime precedent for the same estimator and correction.
