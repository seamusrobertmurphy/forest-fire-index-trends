Subject. The paper concerned spread day thresholds of the six Fire Weather Index System components in the forests of Canada from 2001 to 2021. Each threshold was the component value below which half of the area burned.

Establishes. Wang and others paired the daily growth of fires over 50 ha with daily gridded fire weather across the forested area of Canada from 2001 to 2021. Fire perimeters came from the National Burned Area Composite. Daily growth was mapped from MODIS satellite hotspots, with VIIRS added from 2013, by the method of Parks (2014). Fire weather came from the 0.25 degree Global Fire Weather Indices dataset of McElhinny and others (2020), built from ERA5 reanalysis with the cffdrs R package. Values were interpolated by regression kriging on elevation to 100 m points inside each day's growth polygon. The mean of those points was taken for each component, except the Fire Weather Index, for which the median was taken. The indices were daily, never hourly, and the text described the System's inputs as weather collected at local noon. It named no version and did not mention the 2025 revision. Its citation of Van Wagner (1987) for the System, and of the cffdrs package, indicated the daily noon System that GLC-X-42 labelled FWI1987. The text called the Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC), Initial Spread Index (ISI) and Fire Weather Index (FWI) its fast-reacting variables. The Duff Moisture Code (DMC), Drought Code (DC) and Buildup Index (BUI) were its slow-reacting ones. A generalised additive model of accumulated area burned against each component gave the value at half the area burned. The fit was repeated on 100 random draws of 10 of the 21 years, and the mean and standard deviation were reported. Nationally, over all seasons, the thresholds were FFMC 89.4 (s.d. 0.2), ISI 7.5 (0.3) and FWI 20.1 (0.5) (Table 1, PDF p. 5, printed p. 5). Spring thresholds were higher, FFMC 90.8 (0.7) and FWI 22.0 (2.2), and fall thresholds lower, FFMC 88.6 (0.3) and FWI 19.2 (1.2). Summer thresholds, FFMC 89.2 (0.2) and FWI 19.9 (0.6), were close to the all season values. The thresholds differed by ecozone, with FFMC from 86.9 (1.3) in the Taiga Cordillera to 90.8 (0.7) in the Montane Cordillera (Table 2, PDF p. 6, printed p. 6). FWI ran from 12.9 (1.9) to 25.3 (3.0) between the same two ecozones. ISI ran from 4.7 (0.6) in the Taiga Cordillera to 10.1 (0.3) in the Boreal Shield East. Figure 1 mapped the Montane Cordillera across the interior of southern British Columbia, the region of the Okanagan basin (PDF p. 3, printed p. 3). The text named neither the southern interior nor the Okanagan. Of eleven ecozones, the Montane Cordillera had the highest threshold for FFMC, DMC, DC, BUI and FWI. Its DMC, DC and BUI thresholds were 75.3 (7.6), 442.4 (27.4) and 106.2 (10.2). Its ISI threshold of 7.6 (0.7) was near the ecozone mean of 7.3 (0.7). Its FFMC and FWI thresholds were at percentiles of 52.7 and 58.2, against 64.5 and 68.1 nationally over all seasons (Tables 3 and 4, PDF p. 6, printed p. 6). The authors read ecozone percentiles mostly above the 50th as showing that active fire growth could begin once fire weather passed the median. Fire weather above the 90th percentile accounted for around 20 per cent of the area burned, double its 10 per cent share of conditions (PDF pp. 5 and 7, printed pp. 5 and 7). Fires used 16.9 to 27.4 per cent of potential spread days on average under the national thresholds, and 22.6 to 30.4 per cent under the ecozone thresholds. The authors concluded that median fire weather sufficed for active fire growth, and warned that their thresholds were not switch values. The text cited Podur and Wotton (2011) as reference 13 but did not report its FWI of 19, and named Ontario only in an author's address. It did not cite Field and others (2015). It never named the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System, its danger classes, an FFMC of 91 or an FWI of 30. Its data statement linked a Canadian Forest Service datamart at cwfis.cfs.nrcan.gc.ca.

Bears on

- Methods, "It was also the threshold above which the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System classed the code as extreme [@field2015gfwed, p. 1409]." (qmd L2572, on a Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 91). Adds to it. The text never mentioned the Information System's classes or Field and others (2015), so the attribution was neither confirmed nor contradicted. It found that "FFMC varies the least among all Ecozones (between 86.9 and 90.8)" (PDF p. 4, printed p. 4), so every spread day FFMC lay below 91. The highest, 90.8 (0.7), was that of the Montane Cordillera, the ecozone mapped over the Okanagan (Table 2, PDF p. 6, printed p. 6). Days above 91 there therefore lay just above the value below which half the area burned.
- Methods, "A Fire Weather Index of 30 was that system's extreme threshold for the index [@field2015gfwed, p. 1409]. The same source warned that such thresholds 'will be different in other regions' [@field2015gfwed, p. 1409]." (qmd L2572). Adds to it. The text did not mention an FWI of 30 or an extreme class, so the first sentence stood untested. Its spread day FWI differed by ecozone, from 12.9 (1.9) in the Taiga Cordillera to 25.3 (3.0) in the Montane Cordillera (Table 2, PDF p. 6, printed p. 6). The caption gave these as "FWI System variable values below which 50% of the annual total area was burned by Canadian Ecozone (see also captions in table 1)." (PDF p. 6, printed p. 6). That range supported the warning for spread days, and all eleven values were below 30. The authors added that "The thresholds proposed in this study should not be viewed as switch values." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 8).
- Methods, "A Fire Weather Index above 19 was the fire spread day threshold recommended 'Based on our results for Ontario' [@podur2011spread, p. 506]." (qmd L2572). Qualifies it. The attribution stood, since the text cited Podur and Wotton (2011) as reference 13. It reported no value of 19 and named Ontario only in an author's address. It credited that paper and Wang and others (2023) with taking as threshold the weather at a 50 per cent chance of a spread day. It said "such a technique relies on pre-defined ground truth spread days, which is not universally agreed upon as previously discussed." (PDF p. 2, printed p. 2). By its own area burned method, the national all season FWI threshold was 20.1 (0.5) (Table 1, PDF p. 5, printed p. 5), close to 19. The Montane Cordillera threshold was 25.3 (3.0), the highest of eleven ecozones (Table 2, PDF p. 6, printed p. 6). An Ontario value of 19 therefore lay below the spread day FWI the text found for the ecozone covering the Okanagan. The two thresholds were defined differently, one by area burned and one by spread day probability. Wang and others also took daily FWI from ERA5 reanalysis, not from station records.
