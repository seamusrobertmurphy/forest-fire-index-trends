Question Q2, because it shows how a change in the weather input, interpolated rather than observed, moves the level and bias of each Fire Weather Index System code, with the error carried forward through the autocorrelated moisture codes; and Q1 in passing, for its stationary block bootstrap over days that keeps spatial and temporal correlation, a method the manuscript's season-block bootstrap parallels.

Establishes Jain and Flannigan, Canadian Journal of Forest Research 47, compared inverse distance weighting, thin plate splines with and without smoothing, Akima splines and ordinary, universal and regression kriging with elevation for estimating Fire Weather Index System inputs and outputs at unmonitored sites in Alberta, using 12:00 LST temperature, relative humidity, 10 m wind and 24 hour precipitation from the provincial fire weather network for 2 June to 31 August of 1995 to 2014, with 68 to 102 validation stations per year inside the network's convex hull and leave-one-out cross-validation scored by mean absolute error, mean rank absolute error and mean bias, with 95 per cent intervals from a stationary block bootstrap over daily vectors of all stations. Regression kriging with elevation gave the lowest errors for temperature, humidity and wind, and ordinary kriging with a cube root transform for precipitation. Interpolating the inputs and the previous day's moisture codes and then calculating the outputs beat interpolating the outputs, the reverse of Flannigan et al. (1998) and Tait and Zheng (2005), while carrying the codes forward from interpolated weather alone let error accumulate into a large positive bias in the Duff Moisture Code and Drought Code because of their long autocorrelation times. The saved text is the accepted manuscript with its own page numbering of 47, not the typeset article; the paper reports no trend, no occurrence model and no hourly input.

Quotations

"We find geostatistical methods (Kriging) generally outperform the other methods, particularly when elevation is used as a covariate. We also find that interpolation of the input meteorological variables as well as the previous day’s moisture codes to unmonitored locations followed by calculation of the FWI output variables is preferable to first calculating the FWI output variables and then interpolating, in contrast to previous studies. Alternatively, when the previous day’s moisture codes are estimated from interpolated weather, rather than directly interpolated, errors can accumulate and become large. This effect is particularly evident for the duff moisture code and drought moisture code, due to their significant autocorrelation." (p. 2 of 47)

"The input to the FWI system consists of daily noon observations of temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and 24 hour accumulated precipitation." (p. 3 of 47)

"Based on an early study using data from New Brunswick, FWI values are expected to be representative of conditions within a 40 km radius surrounding a weather station, becoming unreliable beyond a radius of 160 km (Williams 1963)." (p. 4 of 47)

"Last, because each of the fuel moisture codes (FFMC, DMC, DC) track fuel moisture using the previous days’ values, there is a significant temporal autocorrelation in their values. This is reflected in the time lag constants, which represent the time required for each of the corresponding layers to lose approximately 63% (ie. 1-1/e) of their moisture due to evapotranspiration (assuming dry equilibrium conditions). For FFMC, DMC and DC these are respectively 2/3, 15 and 53 days (Lawson and Armitage 2008). This means that any errors in estimation of moisture codes can propagate to estimations made for successive days." (p. 7 of 47)

"Specifically, daily values of primary meteorological variables at 12:00 LST included 2m temperature, relative humidity, 10m wind speed and 24 hour accumulated precipitation as required by the FWI system." (pp. 7 to 8 of 47)

"Over the 20 year period the number of validation stations (total number of stations) varied between a minimum of 68 (106) in 1995 to a maximum of 102 (149) in 2012." (p. 8 of 47)

"To account for both spatial and temporal correlations in the data, as well as any non-normality in the data, we employed a non-parametric vector block bootstrap method to determine the 95% confidence intervals for each of the three metrics. This procedure involves repeated resampling of the data (with replacement) using the stationary block bootstrap (SBB) (Politis and Romano 1994) where each data element in the time series consisted of the vector of observations (or predictions) at all validation stations corresponding to each day." (p. 16 of 47)

"The block length for each variable (see Table 1) was determined by calculating the 95% confidence intervals for a range of block lengths, and selecting the smallest block length above which the confidence intervals converged, ie. were approximately constant. This ensured the minimum valid block length was chosen, particularly important for DMC, DC and BUI, which exhibit the longest autocorrelation times." (p. 16 of 47)

"Overall, in contrast to earlier studies (Flannigan et al. 1998; Tait and Zheng 2005) we achieved the best performance for estimating FWI variables with long autocorrelation times (DMC, DC, BUI and FWI) when meteorological variables and previous day’s moisture codes were interpolated to unobserved locations and FWI was then calculated at the unobserved locations using the interpolated values (ie. the IC procedure)." (p. 19 of 47)

"For all moisture codes, calc-4 lead gave a large positive mean bias compared to the other methods tested. This can be attributed to the propagation of estimation error from the previous day’s codes to the current day’s codes, an effect which is larger for DMC and DC with large autocorrelation times (Table 1)." (p. 22 of 47)

"As the FWI System is a nonlinear empirical model, the IC and CI procedures will in general yield different results, depending on the spatio-temporal characteristics of the FWI input parameters. In the current study, we found that in most cases IC outperformed CI with respect to both MAE and mean bias." (p. 24 of 47)
