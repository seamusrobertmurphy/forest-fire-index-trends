Subject. The paper reconstructed fire frequency and severity over several centuries from tree rings in Douglas-fir and ponderosa pine forests of the middle Stein River Valley, British Columbia.

Establishes. In the middle Stein River Valley, about 20 km west of the river's confluence with the Fraser near Lytton, the study placed 43 plots over 1,105 ha. Two had no conifer of 10 cm diameter at breast height or more and were not sampled. The other 41 lay between 665 and 1,250 m above sea level. Fire scars were dated on 125 trees, and establishment dates were estimated for 1,270 trees. Of the 41 plots, 31, or 76 per cent, held fire-scarred trees, 31 held even-aged cohorts and 23 held both. The 999 dated scars ran from 1562 to 1937, and 81 per cent of them formed between 1700 and 1900. Composited by plot, they gave 305 fire dates, an average of 10 for each plot with scars. Pooled across the study area, the mean plot-composite fire interval averaged 23 years, range 2 to 79, over 1562 to 1937. Over 1700 to 1900, when more trees recorded fire, it averaged 21 years, range 2 to 60. Over 1750 to 1950, the mean interval was 20 years in the middle valley and 23 years on south slopes of the lower valley, 20 km east. Twenty-seven plots, 66 per cent, were assigned a mixed-severity regime, eight a low-severity one and four a high-severity one, and two were left unassigned. The authors limited the mixed-severity finding to their study area, called its extension to the rest of the southern interior a hypothesis and never named the Okanagan. The paper placed the disruption of the low-severity regime in the late 1800s, in a passing remark that named no cause. At two plots, the last low-severity fire was in 1914, and cohorts followed around 1928. Fire exclusion appeared only once, in the Introduction, for some dry mixed conifer forests of western North America (Perry et al. 2011), not for the Stein.

Manuscript sentences checked

1. qmd L2448, "In Douglas-fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii* var. *glauca* (Beissn.) Franco) and ponderosa pine (*Pinus ponderosa* Douglas ex P. Lawson & C. Lawson) forests there, 41 plots over 1,105 ha gave "a mean plot-composite fire scar interval of 21 years (1700–1900)" [@heyerdahl2012mixed, abstract]". Verdict, does not hold. Evidence, the quoted words were verbatim in the abstract on printed p. 88, and the binomials, authorities, 41 plots and 1,105 ha matched it. The abstract attached the interval only to the plots that held fire-scarred trees, "Most (76%) of the 41 plots contained fire-scarred trees with a mean plot-composite fire scar interval of 21 years (1700–1900)." (PDF p. 1, printed p. 88). In that sentence, the phrase giving the interval belonged to the 76 per cent of plots with fire-scarred trees, not to all 41. The Results gave the count, "Most plots contained fire-scarred trees (31 of 41 plots or 76%)." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 92). Intervals came from fire dates composited within each plot, "We composited the fire dates from all trees in a plot into a single record of low-severity fire for that plot and computed plot-composite fire intervals." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 91). A plot without scars therefore had no interval, and the per-plot counts of fire dates left out plots without scars (pp. 92 to 93). Fig. 2 on p. 92 plotted a mean interval for about 31 plots, by my count of overlapping markers. The sentence also left out the place. In a study area section on the Okanagan basin, *there* pointed back to the dry forests of the southern interior. A reader would place the plots in or near the basin. The plots lay in the Stein River Valley, "Our study area is in the lower midvalley, about 20 km west of the confluence of the Stein and Fraser rivers (Fig. 1)." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 90). The source placed Lytton near that confluence and never named the Okanagan. The Stein drained to the Fraser, and the site lay more than 100 km west of the Okanagan basin and about 184 km from Kelowna. Both distances are my estimates from the map grid of Fig. 1a, not figures the source gave. Proposed wording, replacing the paragraph's opening sentence and this one, "Fire history was reconstructed from tree rings over 1,105 ha of dry forest in the Stein River Valley of the southern interior, west of the Fraser River [@heyerdahl2012mixed, pp. 89–90]. In Douglas-fir (*Pseudotsuga menziesii* var. *glauca* (Beissn.) Franco) and ponderosa pine (*Pinus ponderosa* Douglas ex P. Lawson & C. Lawson) forests there, 31 of 41 plots held fire-scarred trees [@heyerdahl2012mixed, p. 92]. Those plots gave "a mean plot-composite fire scar interval of 21 years (1700–1900)" [@heyerdahl2012mixed, p. 88]."

2. qmd L2448, "The regime "was mixed severity over the past several centuries, with low-severity fires most common" [@heyerdahl2012mixed, abstract]". Verdict, holds but the citation should give a page, p. 88, once item 1 names the place. Evidence, the abstract read "We suggest that across our study area, the fire regime was mixed severity over the past several centuries, with low-severity fires most common and often extensive but small, high-severity disturbances also occasionally occurred." (PDF p. 1, printed p. 88). The quoted words were verbatim, but the claim covered only the 1,105 ha study area. The source treated the same regime across the southern interior as a hypothesis, "We expect that this kind of mixed-severity regime was widespread in the southern interior of British Columbia, but evaluating this hypothesis requires that additional studies of historical fire regimes be conducted elsewhere in the region." (PDF p. 10, printed p. 97). As written, *The regime* read as that of the southern interior's dry forests, which the source did not establish. The abstract opened the sentence as a suggestion, while the Discussion stated the result as an inference, "We inferred that historically, the fire regime across our study area was one of frequent, often extensive low-severity fires but some small, high-severity disturbances also occasionally occurred." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 95). The manuscript need not carry the hedge. Proposed wording, following item 1, "Across those 1,105 ha, the regime "was mixed severity over the past several centuries, with low-severity fires most common" [@heyerdahl2012mixed, p. 88]."

Bears on

- Methods study area, "Fire history in the dry forests of the southern interior" (qmd L2448). Qualifies it, because the reconstruction lay in the Stein River Valley west of the Fraser River, and the source called the regime elsewhere in the southern interior a hypothesis.
