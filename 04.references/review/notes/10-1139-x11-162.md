Subject. The paper concerned the Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code as indicators of peatland water table and surface moisture, the first test of the System's deep codes outside the upland pine stands they were built for.

Establishes. Waddington and others measured water table and volumetric moisture for two growing seasons between 2003 and 2009 in eight peatlands at five sites from the Northwest Territories to Quebec. They computed the Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code from noon standard time observations and previous 24 hour rain with the Van Wagner and Pickett (1985) program, starting each year from saturation after three consecutive days of mean temperature above zero, with no overwintering. The slope of water table change on the Drought Code ranged from minus 0.01 to minus 0.11 cm per unit, with means of minus 0.045 cm in fens and minus 0.049 cm in bogs, and a Drought Code of 400 matched a seasonal drop of 4 to 36 cm. Station choice barely changed the Drought Code slopes, but the Duff Moisture Code tracked water table only from within peatland stations. The paper proposed a critical Drought Code from the 40 cm water table threshold and a new peat moisture code. It did not compute the Fine Fuel Moisture Code or any hourly code, and it did not test the codes against fire occurrence.

Quotations

"The slope of the change in WT and DC relationship ranged greatly (–0.01 to –0.11 cm) between sites and years likely due to differences in site-specific peat properties, catchment water supply, and presence of seasonal ice. A DC of 400, which has been associated with wildfire vulnerability in uplands, corresponded to a seasonal drop in WT in the range of 4–36 cm." (PDF p. 1, printed p. 47)

"The FWI System (Van Wagner 1987) uses three codes as indicators of the moisture content of forest floor layers: dead surface litter and other cured fine fuels (Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC)), loosely compacted decomposing organic matter (Duff Moisture Code (DMC)), and deep compact organic matter (Drought Code (DC))." (PDF p. 2, printed p. 48)

"Although parameterized in an upland soil (Van Wagner 1970; Turner 1972), in a peatland, the DMC and DC should be most representative of surface and deeper peat, respectively." (PDF p. 2, printed p. 48)

"DC and DMC values were initiated after the daily mean temperature exceeded zero for 3 consecutive days (as an estimate of the loss of snowcover) and no overwintering of the DC or DMC was used. We recognize that in its operational use, DC is often overwintered to account for long-term drought (e.g., Lawson and Dalrymple 1996); however, in this study to provide a common comparison across sites and years, we chose to start the DC from saturation each year." (PDF p. 2, printed p. 48)

"Relative humidity and air temperature observations at local noon standard time and the previous 24 h accumulated rainfall were used to calculate the DC and DMC components of the FWI System using methods outlined in Van Wagner and Pickett (1985)." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 49)

"In general, the cumulative nature of the FWI System does not meet the standards for ordinary least squares regression, since the independent variables of interest (DC and DMC) are strongly autocorrelated, with a first-order partial autocorrelation function exceeding 0.9 in all cases." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 50)

"For the majority of sites, climate station choice had no influence on the DC–DWT slopes or intercepts (Table 2). While the changes in seasonal WT and DC in general were strongly correlated within sites and years, the slope of this relationship ranged greatly (–0.01 to –0.11 cm) with similar overall mean values for fens (–0.045 cm) and bogs (–0.049) (Table 2)." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 50)

"Correlations between the DMC and DWT were strong only when we calculated the DMC with the peatland micrometeorological station data, as estimates of the DMC using the nearest Environment Canada climate station likely do not capture smaller rain events." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 50)

"Given that the DC represents a similar nominal depth of 18 cm (Van Wagner 1987), a modification of the DC during dry conditions (high DWT) may prove useful in predicting fire danger." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 54)

"If vegetation and surface material interception in a peatland were to exceed the DC interception threshold of 2.8 mm (Van Wagner 1987), less rainfall would reach the WT than predicted, thereby increasing the slope of the DC–DWT relationship." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 54)

"The DC and DMC were developed to track moisture levels in upland jack pine (Pinus banksiana Lamb.) and lodgepole pine (P. contorta) stands." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 55)

"Research in natural and cutover peatlands (e.g., Schouwenaars 1988; Price 1997) suggests that the critical WT depth is ~40 cm below the peat surface." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 55)

"we suggest that the DC in its current form is not suitable to assess combustion risk for peat using an upland forest classification. Consequently, we suggest that there is a need to develop a new peat moisture code (PMC) with a more mechanistic treatment of fuel moisture dynamics (e.g., Viney 1991) to supplement the FWI System moisture codes." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 55)

"Such additional moisture codes and calculation methods were anticipated in the CFFDRS structure by the creation of the Accessory Fuel Moisture System; numerous methods for adjusting moisture codes to specific stands or for explicitly calculating new moisture content values for specific stand or temporal resolution needs have already been developed (e.g., Van Wagner 1977; Lawson et al. 1997; Wotton et al. 2005)." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 55)

Bears on

- Fire Danger Systems, "The optional grassland codes that FWI2025 added" (qmd L2467). The paper is the root of the peatland components. It found the Drought Code to water table slope varying tenfold across eight peatlands and called for a new peat moisture code, so it supports excluding those components from a comparison of the two versions on upland weather.
- Fire Danger Systems, "FWI1987 was the version @vanwagner1987fwi specified." (qmd L2463), the clause on inputs and the sentence "Each code was updated daily from its previous value with no reference period." The paper computed the codes from noon standard time humidity and temperature and the previous 24 hour rain with the Van Wagner and Pickett (1985) program, which supports the inputs. It also reported a first order partial autocorrelation above 0.9 in the daily Drought Code and Duff Moisture Code, which supports treating the codes as cumulative series.
- Weather Record, Gaps and Startup, the script comment "The Drought Code is not overwintered" (qmd L1369), and Season Window, "The window was fixed by calendar in every season" (qmd L2551). The paper started the Drought Code from saturation each year with no overwintering, after three consecutive days of mean temperature above zero, and named Lawson and Dalrymple (1996) as the operational overwintering source. This supports the manuscript's startup and gives a published precedent for the choice.
- Introduction, "Fire danger has been rated throughout Canada since 1970" (qmd L2188), the clause "Their rain thresholds of 1.5 mm and 2.8 mm". The paper stated the Drought Code interception threshold of 2.8 mm from Van Wagner (1987), which supports the figure, and added that the Drought Code represents a nominal depth of 18 cm.
- Fire Danger Systems, "Fire danger was rated with the Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index System" (qmd L2461), the sentence "The System rated the weather against one standard fuel". The paper stated that the Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code were built to track moisture in upland jack pine and lodgepole pine stands, which supports the sentence.
- Weather Record, Season Window, "The window was fixed by calendar in every season" (qmd L2551). The paper named the Accessory Fuel Moisture System and Van Wagner (1977) as the System's own route for codes at other temporal resolutions, which adds context for the hourly method the manuscript describes at qmd L2465.
