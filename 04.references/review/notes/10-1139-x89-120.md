Subject. The paper fitted and tested empirical models that expanded daily maxima and minima into diurnal curves of air temperature, wind speed and relative humidity for fire behaviour prediction.

Establishes. Beck and Trevitt tested the diurnal temperature model of Parton and Logan (1981) and Wann and others (1985), and the wind model of Peterson and Parton (1983), on Australian data (p. 792). They extended the approach to relative humidity and applied it to fire behaviour prediction (p. 792). Between the morning minimum and sunset, temperature followed a truncated sine wave, and from sunset to the next minimum an exponential decay (eqs. 1 and 2, p. 792). The minimum came α hours after sunrise and the maximum β hours after solar noon, with sunrise and sunset found from Julian date and latitude (eqs. 5 and 6, p. 792). Wind speed took either the same sine and exponential curves or a sine rise and a sine fall, switching at the time of maximum wind speed (eqs. 11 and 12, pp. 792–793). Relative humidity was computed from the modelled temperature, with absolute humidity held at its value at the start of each 24 h forecast (eqs. 7 to 10, p. 792). The inputs were the daily maximum and minimum temperature and wind speed, one absolute humidity, the times of sunrise and sunset, and the fitted α, β and γ (Tables 1 and 4, pp. 793, 795). Parameters were fitted by nonlinear least squares, the DUD secant method in SAS, at two sites in open paddocks with no major topographic influence (p. 793). At Goulburn, New South Wales, at latitude 34°45′ S, the data were 10 min means from May 1986 to April 1987 (p. 793). At Jerramungup, Western Australia, at latitude 33°45′ S, 1 min data for January to December 1986 were reduced to 10 min means (p. 793). Days with intermittent or very low net radiation were dropped, judged subjectively, so that only ideal days remained, and the number kept was not printed (p. 793). In a paired difference test, the daily minimum temperature and its time differed from the temperature and time at sunrise at the 0.01 level (p. 793). Because α and β were sensitive to the size and timing of the extremes, these and their times were taken after a five-point weighted moving average, eq. 13 (p. 793). For temperature, α was 0.50 h at Goulburn and 0.63 h at Jerramungup, β 2.05 and 1.82 h, and γ -2.45 and -2.81 (Table 1, p. 793). The root mean square error of temperature was 1.63 °C at Goulburn and 1.72 °C at Jerramungup, and the absolute mean error 1.11 and 1.23 °C (Table 1, p. 793). For wind speed, the sine and sine form gave root mean square errors of 5.22 km h⁻¹ at Goulburn and 6.26 km h⁻¹ at Jerramungup (Table 1, p. 793). The sine and exponential form gave 4.96 and 6.11 km h⁻¹. For that form, β was 2.91 h at Goulburn and 5.60 h at Jerramungup, 75 km from the coast, where sea breezes may have raised afternoon wind (p. 793). Relative humidity was predicted least well, with root mean square errors of 25.68 at Goulburn and 13.87 at Jerramungup, and absolute mean errors of 16.72 and 11.05 (p. 794). These were printed without a unit, and eq. 9 gave relative humidity in per cent. Predictions ran low in the morning and evening and high in the afternoon (p. 794). The authors judged the errors more likely due to the constant absolute humidity than to error in temperature (p. 794). In a paired difference test, observed and predicted absolute humidity differed on average by -1.36 g m⁻³ at Goulburn and -0.25 g m⁻³ at Jerramungup (Table 2, p. 794). Both mean differences differed significantly from zero, at a level not printed (p. 794). Fire danger, rate of spread and spotting distance were computed every 10 min with the equation form of the McArthur forest meter (p. 794). Drought factor was set at 10, fuel weight at 15 t ha⁻¹ and slope at 0° (p. 794). Against observed weather, the modelled weather gave fire danger root mean square errors of 13.47 at Goulburn and 4.07 and 4.04 at Jerramungup under the two wind forms (Table 3, p. 795). Modelled weather tended to overestimate afternoon fire behaviour at both sites, and relative humidity limited accuracy more than wind speed (p. 795). With extreme inputs, both sites' parameters gave an afternoon peak in fire danger, shorter and stronger at Goulburn and longer and lower at Jerramungup (pp. 795–796). For general fire behaviour forecasts, the authors judged one value of each temperature and relative humidity parameter probably adequate (p. 796). They expected detailed local forecasts to need parameters fitted to local data, and left wind to further work (p. 796). The authors judged that their simple functions could not accurately model cloud or synoptic changes in weather (p. 796). Rain appeared nowhere in the models or the text. The models were built into the Kosciusko implementation of the PREPLAN land and fire management system (p. 796). The abstract was on PDF p. 1, printed p. 791.

1. qmd L2188, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly, and a sinusoidal method for expanding daily extremes into an hourly weather stream since @beck1989diurnal." Verdict, does not hold. Beck and Trevitt evaluated an approach already published in the United States and extended it to relative humidity, so the temperature and wind methods did not begin with them. Their temperature curve was a sine only by day, with an exponential decay by night. Their curves gave temperature, wind speed and relative humidity, with no rain, so "weather stream" claimed more than the paper gave, while "hourly" held. Evidence, the paper said "Simple empirical techniques for the prediction of diurnal variations in both air temperature and wind speed have been developed recently in the U.S.A." (PDF p. 1, printed p. 791). It then said "In this paper, we report an evaluation of this empirical modelling approach using two sets of Australian meteorological data and describe how the technique may be extended to include predictions for the diurnal variation in relative humidity." (PDF p. 2, printed p. 792). It credited the temperature model, "employed to predict the diurnal variations in temperature (see Parton and Logan 1981; Wann et al. 1985)" (PDF p. 2, printed p. 792). Its abstract gave the shape, "Solar heating during the daytime is quantitatively depicted using a truncated sine function, and nighttime cooling is characterized by an exponential decay function." (PDF p. 1, printed p. 791) (read from the page image). It placed the use at "temporal scales on the order of hours or less" (PDF p. 6, printed p. 796). Proposed wording, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly. Bears on

- Introduction, "The hourly noon values were biased low because" (qmd L2192). Adds to it, because, at the two Australian sites, maximum temperature came 1.82 to 2.05 h after solar noon and peak wind speed 1.57 to 5.60 h after it (Table 1, p. 793).
- Introduction, "Sunrise and sunset were estimated from" (qmd L2188). Adds to it, because Beck and Trevitt's diurnal curves also needed sunrise and sunset, which they found from Julian date and latitude (p. 792).
- Introduction, "FWI1987 on its 13:00 observation against FWI2025 on hourly weather would confound" (qmd L2200). Adds to it, because, in Beck and Trevitt's tests with the McArthur meter, weather expanded from daily extremes tended to overestimate afternoon fire behaviour at both sites (p. 795).
- Methods, "Under pathway C the 13:00 observation was converted to estimated daily maxima and minima" (qmd L2476). Qualifies it, because Beck and Trevitt computed their curves from actual daily extremes, so their error terms excluded any error in estimating the extremes (p. 793).
- Methods, "Those extremes were expanded to an hourly stream" (qmd L2476). Qualifies it, because the unmodified method was fitted only on ideal days, could not model cloud or synoptic changes, and erred most in relative humidity (pp. 793, 794, 796).
- Methods, "The default offset and decay coefficients of its Table A-1 were used" (qmd L2476). Qualifies it, because the afternoon wind lag differed by at least 2.69 h between sites, and one set was judged probably adequate only for temperature and relative humidity (pp. 793, 796).
