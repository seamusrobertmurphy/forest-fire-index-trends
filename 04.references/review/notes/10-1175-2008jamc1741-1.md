Question Q2, because it supplies a homogeneity test for undocumented mean shifts in a climate series, with or without a reference series, that corrects for lag-one autocorrelation, which is the tool a change of index version or weather input would be tested with, and Q1 in that it shows how an undetected shift reverses a trend estimate and how trend and autocorrelation confound each other.

Establishes Wang, writing alone in the Journal of Applied Meteorology and Climatology volume 47 issue 9 (2008), whose page range the article page text layer did not carry, extended the penalized maximal t test of Wang et al. (2007) and the penalized maximal F test of Wang (2008) into the PMTred and PMFred algorithms, which detect single or multiple mean shifts in a climate series whose errors follow a first order autoregressive process rather than being independent. The critical values were tabulated from ten million simulated AR(1) series for 26 series lengths from 10 to 1200 and 43 or 44 lag-one autocorrelations from minus 0.200 to 0.950 or 0.975, with the autocorrelation estimated from the residuals of the full model fit and its 95 per cent uncertainty range carried into the decision, and the algorithms were released in the RHtestV2 package. PMTred assumes a zero trend and so needs a homogeneous reference series, while PMFred allows one constant linear trend and runs without a reference; neither detects a change of trend. Monte Carlo runs showed the false alarm rate held near the nominal 5 per cent when autocorrelation was accounted for and rose well above it when ignored, and the hit rate rose with shift size relative to the standard deviation and with segment length. The worked examples were monthly station pressure at Burgeo, Newfoundland, 1967 to 1994, and monthly surface wind speed at Nanaimo Airport, British Columbia, 1954 to 2004, where accounting for 11 shifts reversed the sign of the linear trend, with a lag-one autocorrelation of 0.204 in the deseasonalised base series. The paper worked on monthly, not daily, series, tested no fire danger index, and left seasonally varying shift sizes and memory longer than AR(1) to future work.

"Note that, like most of the existing changepoint detection techniques, the PMT and PMF tests are designed for time series of identically and independently distributed (IID) Gaussian errors. However, autocorrelation is inherent in most climate data series, especially consecutive monthly series or series of finer time scales (e.g., daily), which changes the effective sample size of the data series and hence the FAR and detection power of a homogeneity test." (section 1)

"[Note that estimates of either linear trend or autocorrelation could be biased if the trend and autocorrelation are not modeled in tandem, for the two components are often confounded (e.g., see von Storch and Navarra 1995)." (section 1)

"The present study aims to account for autocorrelation in changepoint detection using the PMT and/or PMF test, proposing a practically feasible empirical approach. The proposed algorithms can also handle time series of multiple changepoints." (section 1)

"Such simulations were conducted for each combination of the selected N and ϕ values, for 26 selected values of N (ranging from 10 to 1200), and 43 selected values of ϕ: {−0.200, −0.150, −0.100, −0.050, 0, 0.025, . . . , 0.950} (i.e., each increment of 0.025 between 0 and 0.950)." (section 2a)

"The new algorithms can handle time series of multiple changepoints; they are designed to detect both type-1 and/or type-0 changepoints in time series of IID or AR(1) Gaussian errors." (section 3)

Both are for detecting mean shifts only, not for detecting trend changes." (section 5)
