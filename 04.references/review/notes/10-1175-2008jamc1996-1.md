Question Q1, because it reported trends in a Fire Weather Index System component across Canada and for western Canada and British Columbia over 1901 to 2002 and 1951 to 2002 by least squares regression with an effective sample size for serial persistence and no multiple testing correction, and Q2, because it showed how coarsening the weather input of the Drought Code from daily to monthly lowers its level and its variance and skews its distribution while preserving its year to year variability.

Establishes Girardin and Wotton, Journal of Applied Meteorology and Climatology 48(3), 517 to 533, derived a Monthly Drought Code from the daily Drought Code of the Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index System, replacing the noon temperature plus 2.8 degrees C with the monthly mean daily maximum temperature and placing the monthly rainfall at mid month, and tested it against monthly means of the daily code at four Canadian stations over 1900 to 2006, finding R squared from 0.87 to 0.95 overall but as low as 0.58 in eastern locations in May to July, a systematic underestimate of variance at every station, an underestimate of drought severity at Fort McMurray in all months, and a skew in the monthly series that the daily code lacked, which the authors attributed to light rain below 2.8 mm that the daily code ignores but the monthly total carries. They then computed July values on the 0.5 degree Climatic Research Unit grid of 6171 cells for 1901 to 2002 with no overwintering, examined linear trends by least squares regression with a t test on an effective sample size for serial persistence, on ranked data where residuals departed from normality, and detected regime shifts by the Rodionov method with red noise correction on a 30 year window. Drying was significant in northern Canada over the century, with a shift of nearly 20 units in the northwest since the 1980s, while western Canada showed a decline of 0.37 units per year and eastern Canada a decline, both significant over 1901 to 2002, whereas over 1951 to 2002 fewer than 4 per cent of cells changed significantly and the western and eastern regional trends did not pass 5 per cent. A composite plus scale regression of July values recovered 60 per cent of the variance in Canadian annual area burned and 67 per cent in the annual number of large fires over 1959 to 1999, and regional July values shared 23 per cent of variance with area burned in British Columbia. The paper used no Theil-Sen estimator, no Mann-Kendall test and no false discovery rate correction, modelled no daily occurrence counts, and tested no change over time in the moisture to fire relationship.
