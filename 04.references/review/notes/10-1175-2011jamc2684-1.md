Question Q2, because the paper showed how a change in the observing hour of a Canadian daily weather series, the 1961 move of the climatological day at principal stations to 0600 UTC, shifted daily maximum and minimum temperature and precipitation, and because it rebuilt the daily values from hourly observations to put the two station types on one day, which is the hourly against once-daily input question seen in the weather record rather than in the index.

Establishes Hopkinson, McKenney, Milewska, Hutchinson, Papadopol and Vincent, Journal of Applied Meteorology and Climatology 50(8), 1654 to 1665, documented that Canadian ordinary climate stations observed daily maximum temperature and precipitation over the 24 hours ending at 0700 local standard time and minimum temperature over the 24 hours ending at 1700, while principal stations moved their climatological day to 0600 UTC on 1 July 1961, a difference reaching 10 hours for maximum temperature in the Yukon and 9.5 hours for minimum temperature in Newfoundland. They recomputed principal station daily maxima and minima from the hourly archive for 1953 to 2007 over the ordinary station day, adding monthly corrections of 0.2 to 0.6 degrees C for the gap between hourly and continuous extremes, and recomputed daily precipitation from six hourly synoptic totals ending at 1200 UTC after 30 June 1961. The adjustment changed 10 to 15 per cent of maximum temperatures and 47 per cent of minimum temperatures in the east and 17 per cent in the west, with median absolute adjustments under 1.0 degrees C at Victoria and 1.8 degrees C at Regina, 90th percentiles of 2.5 and 5.7 degrees C, and extreme adjustments of 15 to 20 degrees C. Thin plate spline surfaces fitted to about 2000 temperature and 2700 precipitation stations and tested on 50 withheld stations in 1955, 1965, 1975, 1985, 1995 and 2005 showed the mean absolute error fall 12 per cent for maximum temperature, 15 per cent for minimum temperature and 22 per cent for wet day precipitation, from 1.13 to 1.00 degrees C, 1.58 to 1.34 degrees C and 2.79 to 2.17 mm, while the mean error grew slightly because the remaining residuals ran negative for minima and positive for maxima. The paper cited the finding of Vincent et al. (2009) that the day change biased annual mean minimum temperature by minus 0.2 degrees C in the west to minus 0.8 in the east and mattered for trend analysis, but itself estimated no trend, computed no fire index and applied no changepoint test, and up to half of principal stations lacked the 24 hour programme needed for adjustment.
