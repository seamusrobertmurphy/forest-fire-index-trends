Question Q2, because it compared a fire danger index computed from half-hourly, 3 hourly and single 1500 local time observations at the same stations over up to 20 years and reported how much of the level, the upper percentiles and the count of extreme days the once-daily reading missed.

Establishes Fox-Hughes, in the Journal of Applied Meteorology and Climatology volume 50 issue 8 (2011), whose page range the article page text layer did not carry, computed the McArthur Mark V forest fire danger index from half-hourly airport reports at Hobart (October 1990 to June 2010, 349 421 observations on 7036 days), Launceston (1992 to 2004, 212 332 observations on 4363 days) and Devonport (1991 to 1998 and 2006 to 2010, 183 195 observations on 3710 days), and compared the daily maximum from those reports with the daily maximum from the eight 3 hourly synoptic reports and with the single 1500 local clock time value. At every station and every percentile the half-hourly daily maximum exceeded the synoptic maximum, which in turn exceeded the 1500 value, and the gap widened in the upper tail and in the October to March fire season; at Hobart the 90th percentile of the half-hourly daily maximum was 17 over the year and 22 in the fire season. Half-hourly reports gave between one and two times as many days of very high danger (index 25 or more) as synoptic reports, 58 per cent of the 301 very high events at Hobart and 66 per cent of the 73 at Launceston lasted under 3 hours, 88 per cent of the 17 events reaching 70 at Hobart lasted under 3 hours, and the 1500 reading alone resolved about 53 per cent of Hobart days whose peak exceeded 38. The Launceston record was cut at the July 2004 site move to keep the series homogeneous. The paper estimated no trend, fitted no model and tested no fire occurrence, and it used the McArthur index rather than the Canadian system, so it bears on Q2 as a direct measurement of how sampling frequency changes the level and the tail of a danger index.

"In the case of many manual observation sites with records stretching over some decades, 0900 and 1500 local clock time (LCT) are the only times during the day at which readings were made.1 Of these, only the observations made at 1500 LCT generally provide observations representative of fire-weather conditions during the day." (section 1)

"It is widely recognized by these and other authors who have used 1500 LCT data, as the only available data, in fire-weather climatological studies (Lucas 2010) that FFDI may peak at times other than 1500 LCT. As a result, some potentially significant fire-weather events are missed in such studies." (section 1)

"The current study aims to describe a more comprehensive fire-danger climate at sites in Tasmania during periods of up to 20 yr by computing fire-danger indices from half-hourly airport AWS records." (section 1)

"For all stations and for all percentile values, the METAR value for a percentile exceeds that for synoptic observations. Further, percentiles calculated from METAR and most synoptic observation values exceed those for 1500 LCT (synoptic) observations only." (section 3a)

"In particular, for higher percentile values the gap between METAR, all synoptic observations, and solely 1500 LCT observations tends to widen, especially during the fire season. Thus, the more dangerous the fire weather is, the more synoptic observations and 1500 LCT–only observations underestimate the true situation." (section 3a)

"Figure 3 demonstrates that for most seasons there are between 1 and 2 times as many days of VH FFDI evident on METARs as synoptic observations, further illustrating that an assessment of fire-danger levels based on synoptic (or less frequent) observations will underrepresent the true levels of FFDI at a site." (section 3a)

"For Hobart Airport, of the 301 VH FFDI events, 175 (58%) were of less than 3 h in duration and thus were at risk of not being detected by a synoptic-only observation schedule. In the case of Launceston Airport, 48 of a total 73 VH FFDI events (66%) lasted for fewer than 3 h." (section 3b)

"The above suggests that the higher the value at which FFDI peaks is, the less likely the peak is to be resolved by a synoptic observing network." (section 3b)

"These real and not atypical events indicate strongly that climatological descriptions that are based solely on 1500 LCT observations will miss many such significant fire-weather events." (section 3b)

"At Hobart Airport, for example, examination of observations made at 1500 LCT alone will only resolve some 53% of days on which peak FFDI exceeded 38. This is important to bear in mind not only for safe fire management but for interpretation of climate studies." (section 4)
