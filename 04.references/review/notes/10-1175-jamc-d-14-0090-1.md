Question Q2, because the paper changed one component of the Canadian Fire Weather Index System, the Drought Code, and measured how the level of the code and of the final index moved under the new formulation on the same observed weather, which is the version change problem of Q4 in an earlier form, though it did not touch the 2025 update and did not treat the change as a homogeneity problem in a long series.

Establishes Yang, Uddstrom, Pearce and Revell, Journal of Applied Meteorology and Climatology 54(7), 1523 to 1537, took the Drought Code of the Canadian Fire Weather Index System, defined from noon temperature, a day length factor and a fixed 200 mm saturated store, and proposed two replacements, one substituting potential evaporation simulated by the Joint UK Land Environment Simulator and lowering the effective rain threshold from 2.8 to 1.0 mm for grass, and one computing the code directly from the ratio of observed or simulated volumetric soil moisture in the top 0 to 35 or 0 to 40 cm to the saturated content of the local soil. They computed the code and the Fire Weather Index at 28 New Zealand climate stations over grassland and scrubland on mineral soil, with hourly Aquaflex soil moisture observations, for two overlapping years, 1 August 2009 to 31 July 2010 and 1 February 2010 to 31 January 2011, after a full year of spin up, and scored each method against the observed soil moisture code by mean difference and mean absolute difference. The original code underestimated drought by up to 300 points with a moist bias at 25 of 28 stations and sat at or near zero through winter, the potential evaporation version overestimated drought with a dry bias above 50 at 21 stations, and the simulated soil moisture version had small errors, so that the final Fire Weather Index from the original code was lower by up to six points at Winchmore and three at Paraparaumu, with a yearly mean absolute difference of 0.3 to 1.5 points against 0.5 points or less for the soil moisture version. The optimum evaporation multiplier varied by station and by time of year, about 0.6 for the land surface version and 1.6 for the original, and the code was more sensitive to the choice of method than to soil parameters, whose effect reached 300 points in warm seasons. No comparison with fire occurrence was possible for want of fires near the stations, no trend was estimated, no homogeneity test was applied, and the result rests on two years over grassland.
