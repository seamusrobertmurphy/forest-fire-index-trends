Question Q2, because the paper computed a fire danger index from half hourly observations over twenty years and showed that on 37 of 230 very high danger days the peak stood more than two standard deviations above the day's mean, a within day spike that a once-daily afternoon observation would miss or catch by chance, which is the tail effect of sub-daily input; the index is the McArthur forest fire danger index, not a Canadian Fire Weather Index System component.

Establishes Fox-Hughes, Journal of Applied Meteorology and Climatology 54(12), 2353 to 2363, derived the McArthur forest fire danger index from half hourly observations at Hobart International Airport, Tasmania, from 3 October 1990 to 28 June 2010, selected the 230 days with an observation above 24, and classed as spike days the 37 on which the daily maximum exceeded the mean of the above 24 observations by more than two standard deviations, against 25 nonspike days with a maximum of at least 38 and a departure under 1.5 standard deviations. Composites from NCEP-NCAR reanalysis showed spike days had a more advanced, negatively tilted upper trough with a cyclonically curved 250 hPa jet over southern Tasmania and descent over the island at 850 hPa, with differences in sea level pressure and jet wind significant at 95 per cent on a two tailed t test, and water vapour imagery from 1995 showed a dry band within 5 degrees on 22 of 31 spike events and on none of 15 nonspike events. Spike days clustered in late spring and early summer, differing from the other very high days at p = 0.005 on a Wilcoxon signed rank test, and nine of the 37 fell in November, four of them the season's peak or secondary peak. The paper estimated no trend in the index itself, cited Fox-Hughes (2008) for a marked increase in dangerous springtime fire weather in southeastern Tasmania over recent decades, fitted no occurrence model, and did not compare the sub-daily series with a once-daily one.
