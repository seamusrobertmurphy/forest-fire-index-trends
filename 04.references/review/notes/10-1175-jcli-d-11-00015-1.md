Question Q1 and Q2, because MERRA is the gridded weather source behind the Global Fire Weather Database from which global and Canadian fire danger trends have been computed, and the paper itself records the jumps in its precipitation record at observing system changes in July 1987 and November 1998 and states that reanalysis precipitation is unsuitable for trend study, which is the homogeneity caveat on any index trend built from it.

Establishes Rienecker, Suarez, Gelaro, Todling, Bacmeister, Liu, Bosilovich, Schubert, Takacs, Kim, Bloom, Chen, Collins, Conaty, da Silva, Gu, Joiner, Koster, Lucchesi, Molod, Owens, Pawson, Pegion, Redder, Reichle, Robertson, Ruddick, Sienkiewicz and Woollen, in Journal of Climate 24(14), 3624 to 3648, described the MERRA reanalysis, a global gridded weather record from 1 January 1979 onward produced with version 5.2.0 of the Goddard Earth Observing System model on a half degree latitude by two thirds degree longitude grid with 72 levels, assimilating observations by a three dimensional variational scheme on a six hour cycle with an incremental analysis update, and distributed from three production streams covering 1979 to 1992, 1993 to 2000 and 2001 onward. Land surface fields, including the near-surface weather a fire danger index would draw on, were not analysed but came from the land model's integration of the model's own precipitation, radiation and wind. The authors reported that monthly 500 hPa heights settled after about 1.5 years of spin-up and precipitation after about 2 years, that observation counts rose sharply with ATOVS in 1998 and with AIRS and AMSU-A in 2002, and that global mean precipitation carried a trend or a series of jumps tied to the arrival of SSM/I in July 1987 and AMSU-A on NOAA-15 in November 1998, with further steps in 2001, 2005 and 2008. Precipitation in MERRA fell as persistent drizzle, peaked at midday against observed night maxima, and left too much rain on the canopy to re-evaporate, so that soil moisture skill was comparable to ERA-Interim only after the MERRA-Land correction with GPCP pentad rainfall, at anomaly correlations of about 0.5 to 0.8. The paper concluded that MERRA and ERA-Interim were closer to GPCP than earlier reanalyses but were not yet providing new information on precipitation variability and were particularly unsuitable to the study of trends, and that separating observing system changes from real climate variation was the greatest challenge for the next generation. No fire danger index, trend estimator or occurrence model appeared in the paper, which is a system description and not a trend study.

Quotations

"Reanalyses combine model fields with observations distributed irregularly in space and time into a spatially complete gridded meteorological dataset, with an unchanging model and analysis system spanning the historical data record." (section 1)

"The grid used for MERRA is ½° latitude × ⅔° longitude with 72 vertical levels, from the surface to 0.01 hPa." (section 2a)

"MERRA uses a three-dimensional variational data assimilation (3DVAR) analysis algorithm based on the Gridpoint Statistical Interpolation scheme (GSI; Wu et al. 2002; Derber et al. 2003; Purser et al. 2003a,b) with a 6-h update cycle." (section 2a)

"Since no land surface analysis was attempted, MERRA land surface estimates reflect the catchment model’s time integration of the surface meteorological conditions (precipitation, radiation, wind speed, etc.) generated by the AGCM during the corrector segment." (section 2a)

"The final MERRA distribution is from stream 1 for 1 January 1979–31 December 1992, followed by stream 2 for 1 January 1993–31 December 2000, and then continues with stream 3 for 1 January 2001–present." (section 2c)

"A large increase in the number of observations is seen with the availability of the Advanced TIROS Operational Vertical Sounder (ATOVS) in 1998, and then again with the availability of AIRS and the Advanced Microwave Sounding Unit-A (AMSU-A) on the Aqua satellite in 2002." (section 3)

"There is a trend (or series of jumps and different trends) in MERRA associated with the introduction of SSM/I observations in July 1987 and of AMSU-A data from NOAA-15 in November 1998." (section 7a)

"However, given the given the relative magnitude of the analysis increment in the atmospheric water budget (discussed below) and of the remaining sensitivities to the observing system, we must conclude that even these reanalyses are not yet providing new information on precipitation variability, beyond what is available in the CMAP and GPCP products, and that they are particularly unsuitable to the study of trends." (section 7a)

"They note three deficiencies in particular: (i) MERRA precipitation rates are less intense than observed and tend to appear as persistent drizzle; (ii) MERRA precipitation tends to be highest in the middle of the day, whereas the observations show frequent nighttime rain maxima; and (iii) MERRA incoming solar radiation during daytime precipitation events is not reduced as much as in the observations." (section 7c)

"Observing system changes, which often manifest themselves in reanalysis time series by abrupt variations or discontinuities, can exacerbate such differences. These impacts from observing system changes must be distinguished from real climate variations and pose perhaps the greatest challenge for the next generation of reanalyses." (section 10)

"Without careful bias correction, derived fields like precipitation are not adequate for climate variability studies, much less climate trend analysis." (section 10)
