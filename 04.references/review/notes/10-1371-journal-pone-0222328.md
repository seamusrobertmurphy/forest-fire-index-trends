Question Q1 at the margin, as a station-based trend in a seasonal 90th percentile of a fire danger index over 45 years with ordinary least squares, an autocorrelation-reduced degrees of freedom and no multiple-testing correction across 39 stations, and Q2, because the series were homogenised for changes in wind measurement and the index took its humidity and wind at a fixed 1500 local time observation; the index is the McArthur FFDI, not the Canadian FWI.

Establishes Harris and Lucas, PLoS ONE 14(9), e0222328, pages 1 to 33, took the seasonal 90th percentile of the daily McArthur Forest Fire Danger Index, termed FFDI90, at 39 Australian Bureau of Meteorology stations from December 1972 to June 2017, with the index built from daily maximum temperature, relative humidity and wind at 1500 local time and a Keetch-Byram drought factor from 0900 rainfall, and the series homogenised only for changes in wind measurement. Linear trends were ordinary least squares slopes on annual and seasonal FFDI90, judged significant beyond a two sigma interval whose degrees of freedom were reduced for residual autocorrelation, and no correction was made across the 39 stations and five seasons. All stations but Brisbane Airport rose, the all-station standardised trend being 0.24 plus or minus 0.13 per decade, or 0.96 plus or minus 0.52 index points per decade, strongest in spring and in southern Australia, from 3.13 plus or minus 1.70 points per decade at Woomera to -0.56 plus or minus 0.43 at Brisbane; removing the trends in ENSO, the Indian Ocean Dipole and the Southern Annular Mode by partial regression cut the trend by 27 per cent to 0.18 plus or minus 0.11, so the authors attributed the remainder to anthropogenic warming rather than the Interdecadal Pacific Oscillation. Interannual swings tied to ENSO, strongest in spring, were larger than the trend to date. The study reported no Theil-Sen slope, no Mann-Kendall test, no fire occurrence model and no hourly input.
