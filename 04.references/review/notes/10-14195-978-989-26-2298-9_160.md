Question Q2, because it records a national replacement of one fire danger rating system by another, the Australian version change of 2022, and the reasons given for it, without any treatment of the break it makes in the index record.

Establishes Heemstra, Matthews, Dunstan, Louis and Kington, in Advances in Forest Fire Research 2022, edited by Viegas and Ribeiro, pages 1062 to 1068, described the delivery of the Australian Fire Danger Rating System launched on 1 September 2022, which replaced the McArthur Forest Fire Danger Index and Grassland Fire Danger Index of the 1960s. The new system ran eight fire behaviour models over 23 fuel types on a 1.5 km grid with Bureau of Meteorology weather and produced a Fire Behaviour Index from 0 to 100 and over, with four public ratings set from thresholds in that index. The paper gave the history of the change from the 2009 Black Saturday fires through the 2014 ministerial decision and the social research with 5,430 survey participants, and listed the design limits of the McArthur system, that it could not be updated, covered about a third of the continent and could not take improved weather inputs. It was a programme description, not an evaluation; it reported no comparison of the new index against the old on an observed record, no trend, no fire occurrence model and no method for joining the two systems into one homogeneous series, so for the review it stands only as a documented instance of a version change and its stated motives.

Quotations

"The Australian Fire Danger Rating System (AFDRS) was launched on 1 September 2022, bringing a generational change to the way that Australia calculates and communicates fire danger." (p. 1062)

"The previous fire danger rating system was introduced in the 1960’s by Australia’s first full-time bushfire researcher, Alan McArthur, based on extensive experimental fires. While useful, the system included only two fire behaviour models (dry sclerophyll forest and grassland), was not easily updateable and fires were being experienced that increasingly exceeded its design parameters." (p. 1062)

"The AFDRS starts with eight fire behaviour models representing a range of Australian vegetation types, it captures current fuel information, uses satellite data, integrates weather from the Bureau of Meteorology and calculates fire danger down to a 1.5km by 1.5-kilometer grid." (p. 1062)

"Later, there were also changes made to the ratings for grasslands, causing the GFDI and FFDI to no longer be aligned in terms of the ratings they represent." (p. 1064)

"The design of the McArthur system did not allow for its components to be updated." (p. 1064)

"It could not account for improved weather inputs from the Bureau of Meteorology." (p. 1064)

"In July 2014, Senior Officers and Ministers agreed that the development of a new National Fire Danger Rating System, based on new computational models, was a national priority (Matthews et al., 2018)." (p. 1064)

"This allowed for calculations of fire danger to be applied across 23 fuel types, allowing more precise results (Matthews, 2022)." (p. 1066)

"The Fire Behaviour Index (FBI) is expressed in whole numbers from 0 to 100+. It represents the severity of a fire should one occur under the forecast conditions." (p. 1066)

"The four action-orientated messages are related to underlying transitions in fire behaviour requiring different kinds of actions from members of the community. The fire danger ratings are therefore derived from thresholds in the FBI that encapsulate these transitions." (p. 1068)
