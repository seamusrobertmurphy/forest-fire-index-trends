Question Q3, as the origin of the practice of testing a fire danger index against daily fire occurrence by logistic regression of a binary fire-day on the index and by comparing index percentiles on all days against fire days, and Q2 at the margin, because it fixes the once-daily afternoon worst-case observation as the input of a danger index and sets danger classes from the 90th and 97th percentiles of the record.

Establishes Andrews and Bradshaw, USDA Forest Service Intermountain Research Station General Technical Report INT-GTR-367, 64 pages, described the FIRES program for judging a fire danger index against historical fire records without new field data, using the United States National Fire Danger Rating System as the worked example but accepting indexes from any system including the Canadian one. Each day was classed as a fire-day if one or more fires were discovered, a large-fire-day if a discovered fire reached a user threshold, 10 acres by default, and a multiple-fire-day if more than a threshold number, five by default, were discovered, and the program then compared percentile curves of the index on all days against fire days, large-fire-days and multiple-fire-days, and fitted a logistic regression of each binary outcome on the index following Hosmer and Lemeshow (1989) and Loftsgaarden and Andrews (1992), reporting a chi-square improvement over the constant proportion, a Hosmer-Lemeshow style goodness of fit over ten equal-count index intervals and a pseudo R squared. In the Tonto National Forest example over 1974 to 1994, 7,510 days with 2,560 fire-days, the Energy Release Component for fuel model G gave probabilities from 0.04 to 0.95 and a pseudo R squared of 0.88 against 0.27 to 0.47 and 0.22 for the Burning Index with fuel model C, though the chi-square test of fit rejected both (p < 0.0001). Fire danger classes were conventionally set at the 90th and 97th percentiles of the index, and the report argued that fire activity should enter that choice. The report used daily afternoon station weather only, modelled a binary day rather than a count, carried no seasonal term, no cross-validation and no test of change over time, and did not treat the Canadian FWI beyond citing Fahnestock (1975) on the odds of fire by FWI class in Alberta.
