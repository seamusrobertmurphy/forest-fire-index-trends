Question Q3 at the margin, as a Canadian danger to fire activity relationship fitted at the annual ecoregion scale by stepwise linear regression of area burned on FWI System components and weather, validated leave-one-out, with no daily count model and no test of change over time; Q1 not served, because no trend in the indices was estimated.

Establishes Yue, Mickley, Logan, Hudman, Val Martin and Yantosca, Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics 15, 10033 to 10055, fitted stepwise linear regressions of fire season (May to October) area burned from the Canadian National Fire Database and Alaskan agency reports, 1980 to 2009, in 12 boreal ecoregions of Alaska and Canada on 132 candidate terms, the seasonal means of five weather variables including 500 hPa height and the fire season mean and maximum of the seven Canadian FWI System components for the current and two previous years, computed from daily Global Surface Summary of the Day observations at 157 stations with relative humidity derived from daily mean temperature and dew point. The regressions explained 34 to 75 per cent of the variance (p < 0.001), with leave-one-out RMSE to standard deviation ratios of 0.53 to 1.1; 500 hPa height or temperature led in Alaska and western Canada, FWI components (ISI, DSR, FWI, BUI) led in the four central Canadian ecoregions, and the Western Cordillera, which holds the British Columbia interior, took summer maximum temperature, spring 500 hPa height and the previous year's maximum DMC with R squared of 53 per cent. Applied to 13 CMIP3 models under A1B, median area burned for 2046 to 2065 rose 130 to 350 per cent in Alaska and western Canada and fell 50 per cent in the Taiga Plain, and the authors traced present-day biases to the dependence of the indices on daily rainfall frequency that the models misrepresented. The paper modelled annual area, not daily fire counts, used no count distribution, no seasonal terms within the year and no Theil-Sen estimator, and did not test the index to fire relationship for change over time.
