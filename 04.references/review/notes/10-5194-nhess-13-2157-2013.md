Question Q2, because it compared once daily against hourly versions of four fire danger indices, including the hourly Fine Fuel Moisture Code of Van Wagner (1977), over a twelve day inversion and showed that the daily versions rated two stations alike while the hourly versions separated them, and stated that no hourly method existed for the other Canadian system components.

Establishes Schunk, Wastl, Leuchner, Schuster and Menzel, Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences 13, 2157 to 2167, examined six fire danger indices, the Angstrom index, the Fosberg Fire Weather Index, McArthur's Forest Fire Danger Index, and the Fire Weather Index, Duff Moisture Code and Fine Fuel Moisture Code of the Canadian system, with hourly versions of the Angstrom index, the Fosberg index, the McArthur index and the Fine Fuel Moisture Code, at two stations 2.4 km apart in the Bavarian Alps, Garmisch-Partenkirchen on the valley floor at 719 m and Felsenkanzel on a south facing slope at 1260 m, through the rainless period of 27 October to 3 December 2011 in which seven fires burned at mid slope. A persistent high pressure inversion put a thermal belt at 1150 to 1550 m, so the mid slope station held a mean daily minimum of 4.9 degrees against minus 1.5 degrees in the valley, yet the once daily indices, computed from 12:00 to 14:00 local standard time inputs when the inversion had broken, rated the slope station equal or lower than the valley for most of the period. The hourly versions separated the stations from 19 November on and showed a diurnal cycle in the valley, and the Fine Fuel Moisture Code's 0.67 day lag kept its hourly curve smoother than the instantaneous indices. The daily Fire Weather Index reached 20 to 25 in the spring and summer droughts and only 12 in the autumn drought. The paper was a qualitative visual comparison with no statistics, no trend, no test of the tail of the hourly against daily index, and no multi-year record, and it bears on the version question only in naming Van Wagner (1977) as the hourly Fine Fuel Moisture Code method.

Quotations

"The results revealed that, during temperature inversion, differences in daily cycles of meteorological parameters influence fire danger and that these are not resolved by standard meteorological stations and fire danger indices (calculated on a once-a-day basis)." (p. 2157)

"Additional stations in higher locations or high-resolution meteorological models combined with fire danger indices accepting at least hourly input data may allow reasonable fire danger calculations under these circumstances." (p. 2157)

"The CFFDRS is a semi-empirical system using bookkeeping methods and a relatively large number of equations for its calculation, which are based on 12:00 LST meteorological observations." (p. 2160)

"Hourly values of FFMC can generally be calculated using the methods of Lawson et al. (1996) and Van Wagner (1977). There has been some discussion regarding the relative performance of those methods (Anderson, 2009; Beck and Armitage, 2001); however, we preferred Van Wagner’s (1977) method since it calculates hourly FFMC directly from hourly inputs. Unfortunately, there is no distinct method available to calculate hourly versions of any of the other CFFDRS components; thus, an adaptation of the large set of bookkeeping equations to hourly input is not readily possible." (pp. 2160 to 2161)

"The meteorological data and the fire danger indices described above were plotted for several time periods. Qualitative comparisons between the particular index progressions were made from visual inspections of these plots." (p. 2161)

"The CFFDRS’s Fire Weather Index (FWI, another true fire danger index) also mirrors these events quite clearly. Highest values (20 to 25) are reached for the spring and summer events, whereas the prolonged autumn drought only achieves an FWI of 12." (pp. 2162 to 2163)

"When comparing the indices calculated for the two different climate stations (black and grey lines in Fig. 6), it can be noted that fire danger at Felsenkanzel was almost always equal or even lower than Garmisch-Partenkirchen. This is true even for major, usually the first, parts of the autumn drought period, when the actual fire danger, as described above, was much more severe at higher (e.g. Felsenkanzel) than at lower (e.g. Garmisch-Partenkirchen) elevations." (p. 2163)

"All hourly versions managed to capture the situation much better and showed marked differences between Garmisch-Partenkirchen and Felsenkanzel from 19 November 2011 onwards. Even a diurnal variation was apparent for Garmisch-Partenkirchen in all indices, however of a different magnitude depending on the time lag of each individual index. The instantaneous indices but also McArthur’s FFDI responded very quickly, whereas the built-in 0.67 d time lag of the FFMC led to much smoother curves even in the hourly version of this index." (p. 2164)

"Except for the FFWI, all daily fire danger indices considered in this study, as well as most known fire danger rating systems, are calculated from meteorological values between 12:00 and 14:00 LST." (p. 2165)

"Therefore and because temperature inversions are a regular feature in most complex topographic areas, we state that not only can fire danger calculations from lowland stations be expected to be misleading (Sharples, 2009), but even such calculations based on higher elevation stations may provide spurious results if they are carried out only with standard daily fire danger indices." (p. 2165)

"Further investigation as to which of these or other additional indices are best suited for fire danger rating purposes under the conditions described in this paper are necessary, as our current assessment is limited to qualitative comparisons." (p. 2165)
