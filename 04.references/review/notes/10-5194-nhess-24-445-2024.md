Question Q2, because it replaced the moisture codes of the Fire Weather Index with a different input and handled the change by matching the cumulative distribution of the new input to the old code cell by cell, a homogeneity treatment, and showed that the composite's thresholds still shift because the codes propagate nonlinearly; Q3, because it scored the original and adapted index against daily satellite fire presence over boreal peatlands by percentile thresholds and ROC curves, without a count model; and Q4, because it named the 2025 update of the Fire Weather Index System as a plan, before release.

Establishes Mortelmans, Felsberg, De Lannoy, Veraverbeke, Field, Andela and Bechtold, Natural Hazards and Earth System Sciences 24, 445 to 464, computed the original Fire Weather Index over boreal peatlands of Alaska, Canada, Europe and western Siberia from MERRA-2 noon instantaneous temperature, humidity and wind and gauge-corrected 24 hour precipitation with the Global Fire Weather Database code of Field et al. (2015), including its snow depth and 6 degree mean temperature start and stop rules, and built four adapted versions in which the Drought Code, then also the Duff Moisture Code, then also the Fine Fuel Moisture Code, and finally the whole index were replaced by the water table and surface moisture of a peatland land surface model assimilating SMOS brightness temperatures, after matching the cumulative distribution of each replacement to the code it displaced in every 9 km cell. The versions were scored against 56,709 days of active peat fire presence from the Global Fire Atlas over 2010 to 2018, split at day 130 into 9,025 early and 47,684 late fire days, by hits and misses at the 70th and 90th percentile thresholds of each version's own record and by the area under the ROC curve. Replacing the deeper codes alone changed little, an AUC of 0.70 for both the original and the first version on late fires, while replacing the fine fuel code or the whole index raised the late fire AUC to 0.84, a 14 per cent net gain, and lowered early fire skill, so the authors proposed a seasonally varying adaptation. The paper modelled no counts, estimated no trend, compared no hourly against daily input, and recorded that the Canadian Forest Service Fire Danger Group planned a 2025 update of the system with a new peatland moisture code, which it did not evaluate.
