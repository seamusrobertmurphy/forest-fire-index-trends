Question Q2, because it computed the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and Initial Spread Index hourly from hourly ERA5 weather alongside daily codes, and gave the weakening of the day to night cycle in humidity and temperature as the mechanism by which hourly and once-daily noon input diverge; and Q1, because it trended a fire weather driven quantity over 1975 to 2024 across North America, British Columbia included, by Theil-Sen slopes and the Mann-Kendall test at p below 0.05 with no autocorrelation treatment and no multiple-testing correction across the grid.

Establishes Luo, doctoral thesis, University of Alberta, 2026, 144 pages, supervised by Flannigan, with Chapter 2 published as Luo, Wang, de Jong and Flannigan (2024) in Nature 627, 321 to 327, Chapter 3 under review and Chapter 4 in preparation, set hourly GOES-R active fire detections against fire perimeters to measure active burning hours in 8,720 fires above 200 ha across Canada and the United States over 2017 to 2023, and found one third of active days in the mountain biomes burned 12 hours or more and 14 per cent of nights carried the day's peak radiative power. Fire weather came from hourly ERA5 at 0.25 degrees, with temperature, humidity, wind, precipitation, the Fine Fuel Moisture Code and the Initial Spread Index kept hourly by the diurnal procedure of Van Wagner (1977) and the Duff Moisture Code and Buildup Index at a daily step, averaged over the cells each perimeter touched. A random forest of 500 trees, with biome and month as categorical terms and a stratified three fold cross-validation, predicted hourly burning status at 82 per cent accuracy, an AUC of 0.9 and a recall of 0.79, with relative humidity and temperature the leading predictors, and it was then run over 1975 to 2024 to give potential burning hours in every cell. Annual potential burning hours rose about 36 per cent over the 50 years, significantly in 52 per cent of burnable area and falling in under 0.5 per cent, by 10 to 11 hours a year in California, Oregon and Colorado and 4 to 5 hours a year across 66 to 89 per cent of the burnable area of British Columbia, Alberta and Yukon, with spring and fall rising 57 and 48 per cent against 24 per cent in summer. The thesis modelled no daily fire counts, used no station weather, did not compare an hourly index against its once-daily version on the same record, and did not name FWI2025.
