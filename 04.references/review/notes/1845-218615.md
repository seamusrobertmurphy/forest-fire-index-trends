Subject. The hourly Fine Fuel Moisture Code, three ways of computing it in Canada, and the effect of each on predicted fire growth.

Establishes. The paper compared three hourly Fine Fuel Moisture Code methods. They were Van Wagner's 1977 equations (CVW), the Lawson, Armitage and Hoskins 1996 tabulated diurnal code (BDL) and an equilibrium moisture content method (EMC). On a generalised diurnal cycle built with Beck and Trevitt 1989 for Petawawa, BDL ranged 72.7 to 91.5, CVW 86.4 to 90.6 and EMC 81.8 to 92.0. Fort Smith hourly weather for 1 to 4 June 2007 gave the same ordering. A fire growth model run over 54 days on two Wood Buffalo fires in 2007 gave bias ratios of 1.33 and 1.11 for BDL. EMC gave 1.70 and 1.49, and CVW 2.05 and 1.78. The 1977 method therefore over-predicted area burned by 78 to 105 per cent. Turning calculations off at night cut CVW to 1.51 and 1.29. The constant of equation 87-6 was named as the damping term, and removing it cut over-prediction by 30 per cent. Rain was ignored and no fuel moisture was measured, so the methods were judged by fire growth alone.

Quotations

"The purpose of this paper is to compare methods of calculating hourly values of the Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC) used in Canada." (PDF p. 1)

"Equations 77-1 and 77-6 use an older method to convert to and from the FFMC, F, and the moisture content m. This is referred to as the F scale. The FF scale used in daily calculations (equations 87-2a and 87-2b) has replaced the F scale and is used in the current version of the hourly calculation." (PDF p. 2)

"The drying rates in equations 77-5a and 77-5b use a power of e while equations 87-9 and 87- 10 use a power of 10. Equations 77-5a and 77-5b are equal to equations 87-9 and 87-10, with a conversion factor of -2.303 (which may lead to some rounding error)." (PDF p. 2)

"Another difference (though not formally addressed in this paper) is that the canopy effect on the net rainfall has been removed. In the daily calculations, 0.5 mm is removed from the observed rainfall to account for loss in the overhead canopy. The removal of this term is a necessary step as there is no easy way of tracking this loss on an hourly basis." (PDF p. 2)

"The four iterations of the tabulated values are based on the work originally done by Muraro et al.(1969). Forest litter was sampled up to eight times a day in a dry lodgepole pine site near Prince George, B.C. Van Wagner combined these data with similarly measured jack pine litter data taken at Petawawa, Ontario (1972)." (PDF p. 2)

"Figure 3 shows significant differences between calculated values of the FFMC using the three methods. Values based on Lawson et al. show the largest degree of variation between the peak values of 91.5 in the afternoon and the lowest value 72.7 near dawn. Contrasting this, values based on Van Wagner’s hourly calculations show a smooth transition between night and day with FFMCs varying between 86.4 and 90.6. The EMC method has a larger variation from 81.8 to 92.0. It does not drop as low as that of Lawson et al." (PDF p. 3)

"A final consideration was included in the third stage, where FFMC calculations were turned off at night (that is, setting the FFMC to zero between sunset and sunrise). This is a commonly used technique to compensate for differences between the hourly and diurnal methods (Lawson and Armitage 2008)." (PDF p. 3)

"In this case, the three methods predicted increasing sizes of predicted area burned (97 242, 164 561, and 233 588 ha for BDL, EMC, CVW respectively). Again, Van Wagner predicted a fire 140% larger than Lawson et al., while the EMC approach was 69% larger." (PDF p. 4)

"This would suggest that each method for calculating the hourly FFMC is resulting in over- predictions of area burned: 11-33% for Lawson et al., 49-70% for the equilibrium, and 78-105% for Van Wagner’s." (PDF p. 5)

"Lawson and Armitage (2008) also warned that during extended dry periods, the hourly method will predict excessive fire growth at night, and that the diurnal method produces more realistic values during such times." (PDF p. 6)

"The efforts here show the shortcomings of the current hourly FFMC calculation developed by Van Wagner. Simply put, this method does not produce the expected range of diurnal values of the FFMC. When used in fire-growth modelling exercises, predicted area burned is as much as double that measured by observation." (PDF p. 6)

"The constant term of equation 87-6 controls the dampening of the drying/wetting terms in the diurnal trend. Eliminating this term sets the moisture content into equilibrium with the environment. This approach produces diurnal variations closer to expected values and when used in fire-growth modelling, over- predictions are reduced by 30%." (PDF p. 6)

"Results from this study suggest that the current methods of calculating the diurnal variation of the FFMC are lacking radiation as a physical component in their calculations." (PDF p. 6)

Bears on

- Introduction, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly" (qmd L2188). Supports. Adds that in 2009 the 1977 method held the code within 86.4 to 90.6 over a day while the tabulated method ranged 72.7 to 91.5. It over-predicted area burned by 78 to 105 per cent.
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1.1 stated that "the time lag function used in the FWI2025 for the FFMC has been cut in half"" (qmd L2188). Adds the pre-2025 rationale. Anderson named the constant of equation 87-6 as the term damping the hourly response and found that removing it cut over-prediction by 30 per cent.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The Fine Fuel Moisture Code followed the hourly method of @vanwagner1977hourly with the drying time lag halved and drying allowed to continue overnight" (qmd L2465). Adds that the 1977 hourly equations differed from the 1987 daily ones in four ways. They used the F moisture scale not the FF scale, the constant 0.0579 not 0.581, a power of e not 10 with a factor of 2.303, and no 0.5 mm canopy interception. The manuscript's sentence on cumulative interception should be read against the 1977 method, which had none.
- Study Design, "Those extremes were expanded to an hourly stream by the sinusoidal method of @beck1989diurnal" (qmd L2475). Supports the method as a Canadian precedent. Anderson built his generalised cycle with Beck and Trevitt 1989, with alpha, beta and gamma of 0.88, 1.86 and -2.2 for temperature and 1.0, 1.24 and -3.59 for wind.
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence" (qmd L2198). Adds a Canadian precedent in which hourly code methods were judged by fire growth bias against observed hotspots, with fire turned off at night as a recognised fix.
- Expectations table, "Days above absolute thresholds, FFMC 91, FWI 19 and FWI 30" (qmd L2210). Adds a caution. The three methods agreed by day and parted overnight, so a change in the hourly response may move night values more than the afternoon values the thresholds count.
