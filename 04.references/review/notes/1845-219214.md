Subject. The monthly day length factors of the Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code, the latitude they were valid for, and how they were to be set outside Canada.

Establishes. M.E. Alexander set out how the day length factors of the Duff Moisture Code and Drought Code should be handled outside Canada. In Canada one set of monthly factors served the whole fire-prone range of about 42° N to 68° N. That Canadian standard was strictly valid for one latitude, taken as 46° N from the Petawawa fieldwork behind the Duff Moisture Code. The Drought Code factors came instead from 32 climatic stations in British Columbia between about 49° N and 60° N. Van Wagner (1987) had tested 45°, 55° and 65° N on the 1967 Lac La Biche season. The season mean Duff Moisture Code ran 34.5, 32.4 and 30.0 against 31.9 under the standard, an effect judged too small for special codes. The appendix kept the tables because changing how day length entered the codes would invalidate past calibrations against fire activity. It tested no latitude effect of its own and proposed no adjustment for Canadian stations away from 46° N.

Quotations

"For example, a day in June in British Columbia at 54° latitude has almost twice the drying power as a day in September with the same weather conditions (Lawson 1977)." (PDF p. 2, printed p. 68)

"In the development of the Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index (FWI) System, these seasonal effects were accounted for in the Duff Moisture Code (DMC) by an effective day length factor (Le) and in the Drought Code (DC) by a seasonal day length adjustment factor (Lf )." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 69)

"Details regarding the derivation of Le and Lf were presented by Van Wagner (1970) and Turner (1972), respectively, and will not be covered here, except to say that the derivation of both factors was largely empirical," (PDF p. 3, printed p. 69)

"The effective range of latitude for lands prone to wildfire within Canada is over 25° (i.e., from about 42° N to about 68° N). As presented by Van Wagner and Pickett (1985) and Van Wagner (1987), a single set of monthly values for Le and Lf have been assigned for Canada as a whole (see first row of Table A3.1 and Table A3.2, respectively)." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 69)

"and are, strictly speaking, valid for only one latitude. This has generally come to be considered as 46° N because the fieldwork associated with the development of the DMC and in turn the Le values was undertaken at the Petawawa Forest Experiment Station in eastern Ontario, (Van Wagner 1970), although Valentine (1978) considered the reference latitude to be 45° N." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 69)

"However, the Lf values associated with the DC were based on data from 32 climatic stations in British Columbia (Turner 1972), which effectively cover a latitude range from about 49° N to about 60° N." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 69)

"~46° Nb 6.5 7.5 9.0 12.8 13.9 13.9 12.4 10.9 9.4 8.0 7.0 6.0" (PDF p. 3, printed p. 69)

"Later on, Van Wagner (1987) examined latitudinal effects on the DMC and DC at 45° N, 55° N, and 65° N using theoretical day lengths and daily weather data. He found that the effect of latitude was fairly gradual, and the resultant differences in season average and maximum values (Table A3.3) were not judged serious or great enough to warrant special DMCs or DCs for different latitudes." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 70)

"In 1992, the author of this appendix, as a member of the Forestry Canada Fire Danger Group, made a proposal (subsequently accepted by the group as a whole) that the Le and Lf values for latitudes other than Canada be standardized." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 70)

"For areas at the same latitude as Canada and further north, use the Canadian standard values." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 71)

"In Canada, one set of values has been used over a relatively large range in latitude for nearly 40 years. Furthermore, Van Wagner’s (1987) analyses suggest that the latitude effect is quite gradual." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 71)

"Should a change be made in the way day length is entered in calculations of DMC and DC, any past calibrations between fire danger ratings and fire activity (e.g., Viegas et al. 1999; Dymond et al. 2005) would be rendered invalid. This would be especially significant in Canada, given the number of calibration studies of various sorts that have been undertaken." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 71)

"It would also negate the validity of any fire management guidelines (e.g., preparedness system levels), decision aids and guides, or heuristic rules of thumb." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 71)

"The difference in the DMC values between tabular and point-based calculations of Le (using either daily or mean monthly day lengths) are, in all likelihood, small." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 71)

Bears on

- Fire Danger Systems, "Its inputs were air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed at ten metres in the open and rainfall over the previous 24 hours" (qmd L2459-2468). Adds a fifth input. FWI1987 also took the month, through day length factors for the two slow codes fixed for Canada as a whole.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code dried only between sunrise and sunset" (qmd L2459-2468). Adds a possible latitude part of the version change. FWI1987's monthly factors were strictly valid near 46° N, so drying keyed to local sunrise and sunset would differ at Okanagan stations, which lie farther north. Van Wagner's 1987 test found such effects gradual.
- Fire Danger Systems, "FWI1987 was the version @vanwagner1987fwi specified." (qmd L2459-2468). Adds a British Columbia origin. The Drought Code's seasonal day length factors came from 32 climatic stations in British Columbia between about 49° N and 60° N, after Turner (1972).
- Index Computation, "FWI1987 was implemented for this study in Python and checked in two ways reported in Appendix A" (qmd L2563). Adds constants to check. The standard Duff Moisture Code factors ran 12.8 in April, 13.9 in May and June, then 12.4, 10.9 and 9.4. The Drought Code factors ran 0.9, 3.8, 5.8, 6.4, 5.0 and 2.4 from April to September (PDF p. 3, printed p. 69).
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1 nonetheless claimed continuity" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds an earlier counter-position. The appendix kept the day length tables because changing how day length entered the codes would invalidate past calibrations against fire activity.
- Introduction, "How the System was computed changed what it reported." (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. The appendix treated a change in day length handling as enough to invalidate past calibrations, preparedness levels, decision aids and rules of thumb.
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence." (qmd L2186-2225). Adds that such calibrations were bound to the details of the computation, citing Viegas and others (1999) and Dymond and others (2005).
