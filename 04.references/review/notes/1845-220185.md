Subject. The Fire Weather Index as British Columbia applied it in 1977, from its noon observation and moisture codes to the calibration of provincial danger classes against fire load.

Establishes. B.D. Lawson wrote this June 1977 commentary for a British Columbia slide-tape course on fire danger rating. Observations were taken at noon local standard time, or 1300 daylight saving time. The code from noon weather stood for fine fuel moisture in the mid-afternoon peak, 1400 to 1600 hours. Its time lags were about two-thirds of a day, 12 days and 52 days for the three codes. A spring adjustment of the Drought Code start was applied where winter recharge fell short. Danger classes were calibrated by giving each fire of 1957 to 1966 and 1970 to 1973 the indices of a chosen station. Each class carried about three times the fire load of the class below. Class V began above a Fire Weather Index of 30 in Region 1, but above 46 with a Buildup Index above 90 in Region 3, which held the Okanagan. The text gave the calibration's results but not its station list, fire counts or statistical method.

Bears on

- Introduction, "Fire danger has been rated throughout Canada since 1970" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. The 1977 text dated the Fire Weather Index's introduction by the Canadian Forestry Service to 1969-70 and recorded its use by the BC Forest Service.
- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. In British Columbia in 1977 observations were taken at noon local standard time or 1300 daylight saving time.
- Fire Danger Systems, "Its inputs were air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed at ten metres in the open and rainfall over the previous 24 hours" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports it. The text listed the same four inputs, with wind ideally on a 10 m mast in an opening whose radius was five times the surrounding timber's height.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The System rated the weather against one standard fuel" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports it. The index rested on weather alone so that one scale applied across Canada, with jack pine and lodgepole pine stands as the standard fuel.
- Seasonal Metrics, "The FWI2025 series were summarised to a daily value at 16:00 Local Standard Time" (qmd L2567-2576). Supports it in part. Lawson placed the peak that the noon code stood for at 1400 to 1600 hours, so 16:00 sat at its late end.
- Seasonal Metrics, "FWI1987 'lacks a precise definition for the time to which its outputs are correlated'" (qmd L2567-2576). Adds a British Columbia statement. The 1977 text gave the period as 1400 to 1600 hours without naming the clock, earlier than the 16:00 or 17:00 the report cited.
- Introduction, "The time lag is the time the litter takes to lose most of the difference" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports and quantifies it. Lawson defined the time lag as the time to lose two-thirds of free moisture, about two-thirds of a day for the fine fuel code.
- Introduction, "That function 'limits the impact of high wind speeds on the ISI at wind speeds over 40km/h'" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds a rationale. In 1977 the original spread index was held realistic only for moderate winds up to 40 km/h, the speed above which the 2025 function departs.
- Gaps and Startup, "The Drought Code was not overwintered" (qmd L2553-2560). Adds a British Columbia precedent. By 1977 the regional fire weather forecast unit adjusted spring Drought Code starting values at network stations where winter recharge had fallen short.
- Season Window, "The window was fixed by calendar in every season, 1 May to 30 September" (qmd L2551). Supports the window. Lawson reported Danger Region 1's share of Class V days over the same calendar window, May 1 to September 30.
- Seasonal Metrics, "A Fire Weather Index of 30 was that system's extreme class" (qmd L2567-2576). Adds a regional contrast. British Columbia's 1977 Class V began above 30 in Region 1, but Region 3, which held the Okanagan, needed an index above 46 and a Buildup Index above 90.
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence." (qmd L2186-2225). Adds an early British Columbia calibration. Classes were set from fires of 1957 to 1966 and 1970 to 1973 so that each carried about three times the fire load of the class below.
- Introduction, "In British Columbia over 1981 to 2000, daily fire occurrence was modelled against danger" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds an earlier provincial analysis of danger against fires, the 1977 fire load calibration, which the Introduction's account does not include.
- Introduction, "The Okanagan basin, a populated and fire-prone valley in the southern interior of British Columbia" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. Lawson wrote that the Kamloops-Okanagan regions normally had the most severe fire climate of any forested region in Canada.
- Introduction, "Its terrain was steep enough that a station record was preferable to a reanalysis" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds a caution. Lawson warned that a valley bottom station could not give accurate ratings for plateau fuels 1000 m higher.
- Index Computation, "FWI1987 was implemented for this study in Python and checked in two ways reported in Appendix A" (qmd L2561-2566). Adds a reason for the computed check. Lawson noted that table values differed from computer values through classes and rounding, and called the computer the more accurate route.
