Subject. The first full account of the structure, equations, scales and calibration of the 1970 Fire Weather Index, the version that preceded FWI1987.

Establishes. The text set out the six components of the 1970 Fire Weather Index and the reasoning behind each equation. It fixed the inputs as noon readings of temperature, relative humidity, wind speed and rain over the previous 24 hours, and stated that the index represented the mid-afternoon peak, 1400 to 1600 hours, because the noon weather had been correlated with afternoon fuel moisture and test fires. It gave the time constants of the codes at 70 degrees F and 45 per cent humidity as two thirds of a day, 12 days and 52 days. It adopted the 10 metre open wind, set the Fine Fuel Moisture Code scale as 101 minus moisture content, and warned that scale links between old and new systems could not convert day to day values. It named two calibration studies, Turner in British Columbia and Stocks in Ontario, and recommended regional danger classes set from a historical sample by the number of extreme days allowed. The text contained no evaluation of its own against fire records and no hourly code. The text layer carries OCR spacing faults, such as "fores t", which are reproduced as printed.

Bears on

- Introduction, "Fire danger has been rated throughout Canada since 1970" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. The abstract stated that the Fire Weather Index was issued in 1970 after several years of work.
- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. The 1974 text specified solar noon, which is 13:00 daylight time, and gave the reason, that the noon weather was correlated with afternoon fuel moisture and test fire results so that the index represents the 1400 to 1600 peak. The manuscript gives the time without the reason.
- Introduction, "The Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC) tracks the surface litter" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it, with the 1974 layer weights of about 0.05, 1 and perhaps 10 lb per square foot.
- Introduction, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. A diurnal adjustment scheme existed from Muraro, Russell and Lawson (1969) and a Diurnal FFMC Table from Van Wagner (1972b), so the hourly method of 1977 had a tabular antecedent. The 1974 text said the FFMC drying phase described the afternoon state only.
- Introduction, "The time lag is the time the litter takes to lose most of the difference" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it and sharpens it. The time constant was defined as the time to lose 1 minus 1/e, about two thirds, of the free moisture above equilibrium, and was two thirds of a day for the FFMC at 70 degrees F and 45 per cent humidity.
- Introduction, "The Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code were recalculated hourly, drying only between sunrise and sunset" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. The daily codes already carried a day length factor by month, Tables 2 and 3, because moisture lost by slow fuels depends on the time available.
- Introduction, "That function 'limits the impact of high wind speeds on the ISI at wind speeds over 40km/h'" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. The 1974 text said the exponential wind function was empirical and its validity at very high wind speeds was uncertain.
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1 nonetheless claimed continuity" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. In 1974 the scale links between the old and the new systems were said to be artificial and unable to convert day to day values, because the two systems reacted differently to the weather. The same caution applies to the 2025 claim that an FFMC of 91 corresponds across versions.
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. Turner in British Columbia and Stocks in Ontario compared the index with fire occurrence, area burned and fire size, and the text named two confounders, the frequency of ignition agents and suppression effort adjusted to the danger level.
- Introduction, "Homogenising wind speed at 117 Canadian stations" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports its relevance. The 1974 text said standardised wind measurement was more critical than before if stations were to be comparable.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The System rated the weather against one standard fuel" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports it. The standard fuel was a generalized pine forest, most nearly jack pine and lodgepole pine.
- Fire Danger Systems, "Its inputs were air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed at ten metres in the open and rainfall over the previous 24 hours" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports it. The 10 metre open wind was adopted in 1974 and rain was the previous 24 hours measured at noon.
- Methods, "The window was fixed by calendar in every season, 1 May to 30 September, 153 days, with the calculation started on 1 April" (qmd L2551). Adds to it. The 1974 text left open how much Drought Code to carry over from one season to the next.
- Methods, "Absolute thresholds applied unchanged to all three series" (qmd L2571). Adds to it. The 1974 design set danger classes regionally from a historical sample by the number of extreme days allowed, so a class limit was a percentile of the local record rather than a national value.
- Index Computation, "The coefficient that converted the Fine Fuel Moisture Code to a moisture content was printed as 147.2" (qmd L2561-2566). Adds to it. The 1974 scale was F = 101 - m, so the 147.2 scale was a later change, and the text said the equations were the standard form, that tables could not reproduce them exactly and that reported components were rounded to whole numbers.
- Discussion, "A daily index could not say when the peak arrived or how long it lasted" (qmd L3569). Supports it. The 1974 text said the noon index represented the 1400 to 1600 peak by calibration, not by observing the hour.
