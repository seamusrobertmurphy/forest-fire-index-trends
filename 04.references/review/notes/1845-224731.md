Subject. An operational account of the six Fire Weather Index System components in 1987, their inputs, rain thresholds, time lags, start-up and the hour the daily values stood for.

Establishes. William J. De Groot explained the six components of the Fire Weather Index System to a fire weather seminar in Winnipeg in April 1987. He dated the System's present form to 1970, with the fourth edition of its tables in use. Weather observed at noon local standard time predicted peak burning near 1600 h LST under a normal diurnal pattern. The three codes had time lags of two-thirds of a day, 12 days and 52 days. His prose gave rain thresholds of 0.5, 1.5 and 2.8 mm, though Table 2 printed 1.4 mm for the Duff Moisture Code. Fires began to ignite near a Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 70, with high start potential at 86 to 89. Spring Drought Code starts were to be raised after a dry winter. The spread index was held good up to winds of 40 km/h. The text was interpretive and reported no new data or test.

Quotations

"The Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index (FWI) System is a SUb-system of the CFFDRS and has been in its present form since 1970, with the fourth version of the tables for the FWI System now being used (Canadian Forestry Service 1984; Van Wagner 1987)." (PDF p. 1, printed p. 3)

"The FWI System uses temperature , relatively humidity, wind speed, and 24-hr precipitation values measured at noon Local Standard Time (LST). These values are used to predict the peak burning conditions that will occur during the heat of the day, near 1600 h LST, assuming that the measured weather parameters follow a normal diurnal pattern (Turner and Lawson 1978; Van Wagner 1987)." (PDF p. 1, printed p. 3)

"However, to account for the interception of rain by the forest canopy, the wetting phase of the FFMC is not initiated if the 24-hr rainfall is 0.5 mm or less." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 5)

"Timelag is the time required for fuel to lose two-thirds of its free moisture content with a noon temperature reading of 21°C, relative humidity of 45%,' and a wind speed of 13 km/h (Lawson 1977). The timelag for FFMC fuels is two-thirds of a day." (PDF p. 3, printed p. 5)

"This characteristic of rapidly changing moisture content causes the FFMC to have a short-term memory and only reflects the weather conditions that have occurred over the past three days." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 6)

"The FFMC can be adjusted for times other than 1600 h LST (Van Wagner 1972, 1977; Alexander 1982a; Alexander et ale 1984) to account for -changing _moisture content of the fine fuels throughout the day or to allow for an irregular diurnal pattern of temperature or humidity." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 6)

"Generally, fires begin to ignite at FFMC values near 70, and the maximum probable value that will ever be achieved is 96. At the high end of the scale, a general rule of thumb is that the fuel moisture content is 101 minus the FFMC value." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 6)

"Of importance is the fact that fire starts increase exponentially with an increase in FFMC values at the high end of the scale. In the boreal forest, a high potential for fire starts exists once the FFMC reaches 86-89." (PDF p. 4, printed p. 6)

"A 24-hr rainfall of less than 1.5 mm has no effect on the DMC because of interception by the forest canopy and the fine fuel layer." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 7)

"The DMC fuels have a slower drying rate than the FFMC fuels, with a timelag of 12 days. Due to the slower drying rate, the length of daily drying time is important. Therefore, a seasonal day-length factor has been incorporated into the drying phase of the DMC." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 7)

"The DMC is often used to assist in predicting the probability of lightning fire starts (Fig. 4) since lightning strikes usually result in fires smoldering in the duff layer." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 7)

"A 24-hr rainfall greater than 2.8 mm is required to affect the moisture content due to interception by upper fuel layers and the forest canopy." (PDF p. 5, printed p. 7)

"The DC fuels have a very slow drying rate, with a timelag of 52 days. Therefore, a seasonal day-length factor is also incorporated in the drying phase." (PDF p. 6, printed p. 8)

"Because of the slow drying-rate of DC fuels, the amount of overwinter precipitation is critical to calculating spring starting values. If there has not been sufficient overwinter precipitation to recharge moisture levels in the deep organic layers, then an upward adjustment of the DC in the spring must be done to reflect the drier conditions (Turner and Lawson 1978; Alexander 1982b, 1983)." (PDF p. 6, printed p. 8)

"Generally, a 13 km/h increase in wind speed will double the lSI value. The lSI is accepted as a good indicator of fire spread in open light-fuel stands with wind speeds up to 40 km/h." (PDF p. 6, printed p. 8)

"24-hour Rainfall Threshold (rom) 0.5 1.4 2.8" (PDF p. 7, printed p. 9)

"This value may be adjusted upwards to account for lack of sufficient overwinter precipitation." (PDF p. 7, printed p. 9)

"For instance, to account for isolated rainfall at a weather station, the fire manager must also calculate a second set of FWI System values using no-rain to represent areas which did not receive any precipitation (the calculation using the actual rainfall at the weather station is used for the following days calculation)." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 11)

"A recalculation of the FWI System would also have to be done if normal diurnal condi tions did not occur between noon and the peak burning period." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 11)

"By comparing fire activity (fire starts, rate of spread, difficulty of control, etc.) to the values produced by the FWI System, fire managers will gain an expertise in interpreting the FWI System." (PDF p. 10, printed p. 12)

Bears on

- Introduction, "Fire danger has been rated throughout Canada since 1970" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. De Groot dated the System's present form to 1970, with the fourth edition of its tables in use in 1987.
- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it in standard time. De Groot gave the observation as noon local standard time, which falls at 13:00 under daylight saving.
- Seasonal Metrics, "The FWI2025 series were summarised to a daily value at 16:00 Local Standard Time" (qmd L2567-2576). Supports the hour directly. De Groot wrote that noon values predicted peak burning near 1600 h LST, and that the fine fuel code could be adjusted for other times.
- Seasonal Metrics, "FWI1987 'lacks a precise definition for the time to which its outputs are correlated'" (qmd L2567-2576). Contradicts it in part. In 1987 De Groot wrote that noon values "are used to predict the peak burning conditions that will occur during the heat of the day, near 1600 h LST" (PDF p. 1, printed p. 3), citing Turner and Lawson (1978) and Van Wagner (1987).
- Introduction, "The time lag is the time the litter takes to lose most of the difference" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports and quantifies it. De Groot defined the time lag as the time to lose two-thirds of free moisture on a standard day, two-thirds of a day for the fine fuel code.
- Fire Danger Systems, "Canopy interception of the first 0.5 mm of rain became cumulative within a rain event" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports the 1987 baseline. De Groot gave the fine fuel code's canopy interception as 24-hour rain of 0.5 mm or less.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The 1.5 mm and 2.8 mm rain thresholds of the Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code were applied the same way" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports both values in his prose. His Table 2 printed "24-hour Rainfall Threshold (rom) 0.5 1.4 2.8" (PDF p. 7, printed p. 9), 1.4 mm rather than 1.5 mm.
- Introduction, "That function 'limits the impact of high wind speeds on the ISI at wind speeds over 40km/h'" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds the original limit. De Groot held the 1987 spread index a good indicator only in open light-fuel stands with winds up to 40 km/h.
- Gaps and Startup, "The Drought Code was not overwintered" (qmd L2557). Contrasts with 1987 guidance. De Groot wrote that the spring Drought Code had to be raised when overwinter precipitation had not recharged the deep organic layers.
- Gaps and Startup, "Each station-season started from a Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 85, a Duff Moisture Code of 6" (qmd L2557). Supports the first two values. Table 2 listed spring starting values of 85 and 6, and the Drought Code value is missing from the layer.
- Index Computation, "The coefficient that converted the Fine Fuel Moisture Code to a moisture content was printed as 147.2" (qmd L2561-2566). Adds history. By 1987 the relation of moisture content to 101 minus the code survived as a rule of thumb at the high end of the scale.
- Seasonal Metrics, "A Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 91 was taken from section 4.1" (qmd L2567-2576). Adds context. De Groot put the maximum probable code at 96 and high start potential at 86 to 89, so 91 lay where starts rose exponentially.
- Introduction, table of expectations row "Daily lightning, person-caused and all-cause ignitions" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds a component choice. De Groot named the Duff Moisture Code as the component often used to predict lightning fire starts, after Martell (1976), because lightning fires smoulder in duff.
- Discussion, "A daily index could not say when the peak arrived or how long it lasted" (qmd L3569). Supports it. De Groot required a recalculation when the afternoon departed from the normal diurnal pattern, valid for that afternoon only.
