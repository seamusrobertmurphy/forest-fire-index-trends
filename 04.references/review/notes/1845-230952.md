Subject. The national standards for the weather observations behind the Fire Weather Index, covering observation time, instruments, station siting, missing days, start-up values and the overwinter Drought Code adjustment.

Establishes. J.A. Turner and B.D. Lawson set national standards for fire weather observation in May 1978, updating Williams (1963). The index rested on one daily observation at noon local standard time, 1300 daylight saving time. It replaced the older sun noon with the clock hour, accurate within 15 minutes. The noon value represented the midafternoon peak under a normal diurnal pattern, and the index was not designed to show hour by hour change. Rain thresholds were 0.6, 1.5 and 2.9 mm. Codes started at 85, 6 and 15 on the third day after snowmelt. Where snow was rare, the start followed three days of noon temperatures of 12 °C or more. Missing days were to be estimated, never skipped. A stored moisture equation could raise the spring Drought Code after a dry winter. Stations were to avoid sheltered valleys and ridge tops, with wind at 10 m in the open. The guide set standards and reported no test of its own.

Bears on

- Introduction, "Fire danger has been rated throughout Canada since 1970" (qmd L2186-2225). Qualifies the date. Turner and Lawson put the index in operational use across Canada from 1971, and the System's development in its present form from 1968.
- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it and gives the reason. The 1978 standard was noon local standard time, 1300 daylight time, late enough to indicate the afternoon peak and early enough for planning. The clock hour replaced the sun noon of Williams (1963) for communications and data handling, within 15 minutes.
- Fire Danger Systems, "Its inputs were air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed at ten metres in the open and rainfall over the previous 24 hours" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports it. The guide named noon temperature and humidity, a 10-minute mean wind at 10 m over open level ground and 24-hour rainfall, with the month setting day length.
- Introduction, "Their rain thresholds of 1.5 mm and 2.8 mm were applied to rain accumulated within a rain event" (qmd L2186-2225). Differs on the Drought Code value. The guide's Table 1 read "CODE 24-HOUR RAINFALL THRESHOLD mm FFMC 0.6 DMC 1.5 DC 2.9" (PDF p. 7, printed p. 7), so 2.8 mm was not the threshold in every published version.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The 1.5 mm and 2.8 mm rain thresholds of the Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code were applied the same way" (qmd L2459-2468). Differs in the same way. The 1978 national guide printed the Drought Code threshold as 2.9 mm, so the 2.8 mm value belongs to the 1987 equations.
- Index Computation, "The coefficient that converted the Fine Fuel Moisture Code to a moisture content was printed as 147.2" (qmd L2561-2566). Adds history. The 1978 guide still called "the standard conversion: Moisture Content = 101 - FFMC" (PDF p. 7, printed p. 7) the conversion, so 147.2 was not the only form in print.
- Index Computation, "FWI1987 was implemented for this study in Python and checked in two ways" (qmd L2563). Adds the earlier reference code. In 1978 the regional networks computed the index daily with the Fortran IV program of Van Wagner and Pickett (1975) for the 1976 metric version.
- Seasonal Metrics, "FWI1987 'lacks a precise definition for the time to which its outputs are correlated'" (qmd L2567-2576). Supports it. The guide tied the daily value only to the midafternoon peak under a normal diurnal pattern, naming no hour, and allowed local revisions up to 1600 h LST.
- Discussion, "A daily index could not say when the peak arrived or how long it lasted" (qmd L3569). Supports it in the System's own words. The guide stated that with one value per day the System was not designed to indicate hour by hour changes.
- Discussion, "It was not to be expected at a site with a different diurnal range" (qmd L3561). Supports the caution. The guide said the index assumed an average diurnal pattern from late morning to late afternoon, and that valley wind stations posed special problems.
- Introduction, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. The guide said the code could be computed for every hour from hourly readings with Van Wagner's 1977 program, recommended for valley winds and other unusual diurnal patterns.
- Gaps and Startup, "Each station-season started from a Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 85, a Duff Moisture Code of 6 and a Drought Code of 15" (qmd L2557). Supports the values and dates them to 1978. Where snow was not significant, the guide instead started after three days of noon temperatures of 12 °C or more, with slow codes set from days since rain.
- Season Window, "It replaced the Canadian start rule, which began the calculation three days after the snow cover disappeared" (qmd L2551). Supports it. The three-day snowmelt rule stood in the 1978 national guide, which put the fire season at part of April, May through September and part of October.
- Gaps and Startup, "The Drought Code was not overwintered" (qmd L2557). Adds the 1978 procedure. A stored moisture index with carry-over fractions of 1.00, 0.75 or 0.50 and winter precipitation effectiveness of 0.50, 0.75 or 0.90 raised spring Drought Code starts. The same guide also said codes could generally start from zero each spring.
- Gaps and Startup, "Runs of missing temperature, humidity or wind up to 24 hours were filled by linear interpolation and missing precipitation was set to zero" (qmd L2555). Contrasts with the 1978 practice for a daily record. The guide had missed days estimated from records, averages or nearby stations, with the next day's measured rain shared over the missed days.
- Introduction, "Its terrain was steep enough that a station record was preferable to a reanalysis" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds a caution. The guide said a British Columbia station's area was best judged by local knowledge, and that valley bottom stations related poorly to ridge tops a few kilometres away.
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence." (qmd L2186-2225). Adds an early British Columbia result. The guide cited a 10-year provincial study, Lawson (1972), in which index values gave useful information on fires up to 100 km from the station.
