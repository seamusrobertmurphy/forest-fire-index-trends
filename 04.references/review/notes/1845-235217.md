Subject. A calibration of the Fire Weather Index Tables against five years of Alberta fire records, with danger classes set from the frequency of days and the relation of each component to fire occurrence, fire size and suppression cost.

Establishes. Over 35,000 daily observations from 49 fire weather stations, each covering about 8550 km2, over May to October of 1965 to 1969 were run to daily indices, and 2,060 fires were matched to the nearest station. Classes were set so that 30, 40, 20, 7 and 3 per cent of days fell in Low to Extreme, which put Extreme at a Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 92 and above and a Fire Weather Index above 30. Relative to the Moderate class the daily fire rate rose about 4, 13 and 30 fold in High, Very High and Extreme. Extreme days were 4 per cent of days, 16 per cent of fires, 20 per cent of area and 28 per cent of cost. Every index except the Buildup Index tracked man caused fires better than lightning fires, 17 fold against 3 fold across the Fine Fuel Moisture Code classes, and lightning incidence was nearly flat across the Fire Weather Index. Over 70 per cent of fires above 202 ha started at Very High or Extreme, against 35 per cent of all fires. The text warned that changed fire control since 1965 to 1969 might have made the correlations imprecise, and that the same index value meant different occurrence in different seasons. It computed no hourly index.

Bears on

- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. The Alberta calibration used 49 stations, over 35,000 station days and 2,060 fires over 1965 to 1969, matched each fire to its nearest station, and found the daily fire rate 4, 13 and 30 times the Moderate rate in High, Very High and Extreme.
- Table of expectations, "Daily lightning, person-caused and all-cause ignitions | Positive association with daily danger; weaker for lightning without strike data" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it directly. Every component except the Buildup Index tracked man caused fires better than lightning fires, the Fine Fuel Moisture Code 17 fold against 3 fold, and lightning incidence was nearly constant across the Fire Weather Index.
- Introduction, "In Ontario over 1976 to 2019, the one Canadian test asked whether that relationship stayed the same" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. The Alberta report stated in 1977 that changes in fire control since 1965 to 1969 might have made its correlations imprecise, and that the same index value gave different fire occurrence in different seasons, but it tested neither.
- Methods, "A Fire Weather Index of 30 was that system's extreme class" (qmd L2571). Adds the origin. In Alberta over 1965 to 1969 a Fire Weather Index above 30 occurred on 3 per cent of days and was set as the Extreme limit on that frequency, with the Fine Fuel Moisture Code Extreme limit at 92 and Very High at 89 to 91, so these absolute classes were percentiles of one province's record.
- Methods, "Absolute thresholds applied unchanged to all three series" and the percentile families (qmd L2571). Adds to it. The 1977 classes were derived from an arbitrary percentage frequency of days, the same device as the manuscript's per series percentiles.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The System rated the weather against one standard fuel" (qmd L2459-2468). Supports it. The components conveyed fuel moisture and fire behaviour in a standard fuel type, so calibration against fire records was held essential.
- Discussion, "A daily index could not say when the peak arrived or how long it lasted" (qmd L3569). Supports it. The components were designed to predict fire weather severity on a given afternoon.
- Introduction, "The Okanagan basin ... had a long instrumented fire weather record" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds context. In Alberta each of 49 stations stood for about 8550 km2.
