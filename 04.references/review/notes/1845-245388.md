Subject. The text concerned how Ontario's adjective classes for the six System outputs were set by frequency, and a replacement tying each output to one physical process.

Establishes. Hanes, Wotton, McFayden and Jurko proposed Ontario classes for the six Fire Weather Index System outputs, each tied to one physical process. Stocks (1971, 1974) had set the existing classes by frequency, putting 2 per cent of fire season days in Extreme. Ontario's current Extreme began at a Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 91 and a Fire Weather Index of 23. The proposed classes rested on flaming ignition, lightning fires per strike, depth of burn, C-3 spread rate, fuel consumption and head fire intensity. Their Extreme began above 90 and 20 for the same two outputs. At 75 Ontario station points with 32,191 fires over 1990 to 2019, the old boundaries no longer held their intended frequencies. They wrote that the next-generation changes aimed to reproduce the existing range of outputs, though frequencies might differ. They tested no British Columbia station, compared no versions and proposed no FWI2025 class.

"The Fire Weather Index (FWI) System, introduced in Canada in 1969, was developed to provide estimates of daily potential fire danger based on weather observations taken each day at 12:00 pm local standard time (Van Wagner 1987). These observations of temperature, relative humidity (both at 1.5 m height in a sheltered location), wind speed (at 10 m height in a forest clearing), and 24-hr accumulated precipitation are used to calculate three moisture codes which represent the moisture content of forest floor fuel layers." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2)

"Each code is important to different aspects of fire activity in typical boreal forest fuel types: litter and other cured fine fuels (Fine Fuel Moisture Code, FFMC), loosely compacted shallow organic matter of moderate depth (Duff Moisture Code, DMC) and deep, compact organic layers (Drought Code, DC)." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2)

"Lastly, the ISI and BUI are combined, following an approach similar to Byram (1959) to provide a unitless indicator of fireline intensity. This final index of the system, FWI, is scaled to increase similarly with flame length." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2)

Bears on

- Introduction, "Fire danger has been rated throughout Canada since 1970" (qmd L2186-2225). The report contradicted the year, dating the System's national introduction to 1969 on its abstract page and in its first sentence. It read "The Fire Weather Index (FWI) System, introduced in Canada in 1969" (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2).
- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2186-2225). The report supported the hour in standard time, as "weather observations taken each day at 12:00 pm local standard time" (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2), citing Van Wagner (1987). It gave no reason for the hour.
- Fire Danger Systems, "Its inputs were air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed at ten metres in the open and rainfall over the previous 24 hours" (qmd L2459-2468). The report supported the four inputs and placed temperature and humidity at 1.5 m in a sheltered location. It differed on exposure, putting the 10 m wind "in a forest clearing" rather than in the open.
- Introduction, "The Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC) tracks the surface litter" (qmd L2186-2225). The report supported the three layers, describing them as litter and cured fine fuels, shallow loosely compacted organic matter and deep compact organic layers of a standard pine forest.
- Introduction, "From these it computes the Initial Spread Index (ISI), the Buildup Index and the Fire Weather Index (FWI), a single rating of fire danger." (qmd L2186-2225). The report qualified the last phrase, calling the Fire Weather Index a unitless indicator of fireline intensity scaled like flame length. It also said "Communicating the complete picture of fire danger is not possible with a single number or FWI System indicator." (PDF p. 10, printed p. 3).
- Introduction, "The Canadian Forest Service Fire Danger Group replaced the 1987 System" (qmd L2186-2225). The report added the timeline, recording in 2021 that the Canadian Forest Service was already changing the System's calculations for the next generation. Its sustained flaming model came from that unpublished next-generation work.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The System rated the weather against one standard fuel" (qmd L2459-2468). The report supported it, saying the mature closed-canopy pine standard "will remain the standard fuel type in proposed updates to the System" (PDF p. 14, printed p. 7). It added that outputs were "intended to be interpreted regionally to account for local differences" (PDF p. 37, printed p. 30).
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1 nonetheless claimed continuity" (qmd L2186-2225). The report gave the same aim earlier, from Canadian Forest Service authors, that the changes were planned "to reproduce the general existing range in observed outputs" (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2). It compared no versions, which was consistent with the claim that no published study had done so.
- Threshold Positions, "Over the reference period of 1991 to 2020" (qmd L2833). The report anticipated the finding that the value held and the count did not. It warned that "some differences in the frequency of occurrence of different codes or index values may result" (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2).
- Seasonal Metrics, "It was also the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System's extreme class for the code" (qmd L2571). The report added that classes were set province by province, by frequency and with different numbers of classes, and it never named the Canadian Wildland Fire Information System. Ontario's Extreme began at a Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 91 and a Fire Weather Index of 23, placing an index of 30 in Extreme and 19 in High.
- Seasonal Metrics, "A Fine Fuel Moisture Code of 91 was taken from section 4.1" (qmd L2571). The report's proposed Extreme for the code was inconsistent at 90, "Extreme ≥90" in Table 5 (PDF p. 19, printed p. 12) but above 90 in the text and Table 6B. A code of 91 fell in Extreme under both the current and the proposed Ontario classes either way.
- Introduction, "Under thresholds set as percentiles within each series over a common reference period, that difference should largely vanish" (qmd L2186-2225). The report added a precedent, Ontario classes set once as frequencies over 1963 to 1968 and then held fixed in index units. Over 1990 to 2019 the Fire Weather Index Extreme took 1 to 6 per cent of days across the middle 80 per cent of stations. The Fine Fuel Moisture Code Extreme took 3 to 9 per cent, against the 2 per cent intended.
- Introduction, table of expectations, row "Days above each series' own 90th and 95th percentiles", "Percentile thresholds move with the series" (qmd L2186-2225). The report supported the reasoning, saying frequency-based classes "should be regularly evaluated with new fire weather data and updated when needed" (PDF p. 37, printed p. 30).
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence." (qmd L2186-2225). The report added Ontario's record, where Stocks (1971) found 13 per cent of 3,159 fires on the 2 per cent of Extreme days, a relative fire occurrence of 6.5. It matched 32,191 fires over 1990 to 2019 to 75 station points within 50 km, by class on the start date. The abstract gave the period as 1990 to 2018.
- Introduction, "In Ontario over 1976 to 2019, the one Canadian test asked whether that relationship stayed the same" (qmd L2186-2225). The report did not contradict it, because it compared class frequencies across decades without testing whether fire occurrence related to danger in the same way.
- Introduction, table of expectations, row "Daily lightning, person-caused and all-cause ignitions", "Positive association with daily danger; weaker for lightning without strike data" (qmd L2186-2225). The report supported the reasoning. Lightning fires per strike rose with the Duff Moisture Code, from about 1 in 1,000 at a code of 10 to 1 in 50 at 55. A lightning count without strike data would therefore mix strike numbers with fuel dryness, and the report called its ratios "more descriptive than predictive" (PDF p. 24, printed p. 17).
- Season Window, "The window was fixed by calendar in every season, 1 May to 30 September" (qmd L2551). The report added a precedent for confining the analysis to the station season, since Stocks (1974) excluded "spring and fall fires when weather stations were not running" (PDF p. 11, printed p. 4).
- Discussion, "A daily index could not say when the peak arrived or how long it lasted." (qmd L3569). The report supported it for FWI1987, describing the outputs as indicators for "any particular day". It sent users needing "the expected diurnal change in fire behaviour" (PDF p. 38, printed p. 31) to the Fire Behaviour Prediction System.
