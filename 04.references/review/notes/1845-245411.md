Subject. The text set out the design of CFFDRS-2025, the System's next generation, and why the Fire Weather Index System's daily inputs were to change.

Establishes. The Canadian Forest Service Fire Danger Group described CFFDRS-2025, due from a 2020 to 2025 research program and phased in from 2025. The System, not substantially updated since 1992, was to keep the Fire Weather Index System's core structure, interpretation and closed canopy conifer reference fuel. Basic continuity of inputs was a stated goal. Daily codes were to use the day's maximum temperature, minimum humidity and wind at the time of maximum temperature, not the 13:00 observation. The stated reasons were that a noon reading misjudged peak danger in unstable weather and that forecasts had made it less relevant. Daily, hourly and diurnally adjusted Fine Fuel Moisture Codes disagreed, a long-standing concern of users. Latitude, longitude and date were to replace the month input, giving explicit day length. Overwintering and station start-up were also to improve. The report gave no equations, ran no tests and set no danger classes.

Therefore, in the CFFDRS-2025, the core structure and interpretation of the FWI System will remain unchanged (Figure 5). "The convention of using noon standard time weather observations as an early indicator (i.e., a forecast) of the weather during the peak burning period later in the day in the FWI System is a legacy of a practice introduced in the 1930s (Beall 1939). In the absence of other observations or forecasts, it was a way to inform the afternoon’s fire suppression planning using the principle of persistence. However, this practice can under or overestimate fire danger considerably at the peak burning period in unstable weather conditions (e.g., the passage of a cold front in the afternoon bringing strong winds or rain)." (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23)

"It is also less relevant now that multi-day weather forecasts (including numerical weather prediction model outputs) are ubiquitous in wildfire management; daily preparedness planning at the district or regional level typically takes place in the morning of a day and/or on the previous afternoon with forecasted weather." (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23)

"Thus, the fuel moisture content calculation methods within the new FWI System will be modified to rely on the forecast or observed daily maximum temperature, minimum humidity, and the wind speed at the time of maximum temperature to better represent the peak burning conditions. (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23)

"The change from noon (standard time) weather observations to the use of daily maximum and minimums will also provide a clearer linkage between typical weather forecast quantities and the new FWI System calculations; this change to the calculations will also allow hourly data, where it is available, to be used consistently the FWI System calculation." The discrepancy in predictions from these three different methods has long been a concern raised by users of the CFFDRS." (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23)

"While the final bookkeeping estimate of moisture content will be shifted to the end of the drying period (i.e., late in a typical day or early the subsequent morning), this will not affect the timing of the operational planning activity, which is usually based on forecast information and should be transparent to fire management agencies that use automated calculation methods." Bears on

- Introduction, "Fire danger has been rated throughout Canada since 1970" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported the year as that of the first edition. Its Box 1 recorded a provisional version in 1969 and "the first edition of the FWI System in the subsequent year" (PDF p. 12, printed p. 6). That reconciled the 1969 date of Hanes et al. (2021).
- Introduction, "It is the part of the Canadian Forest Fire Danger Rating System that models fuel moisture and fire behaviour from weather alone" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported it, calling the FWI System "a complete system for fire danger rating assessment based on weather observations" (PDF p. 10, printed p. 4).
- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported the hour and gave its origin. It traced the noon standard time reading to a 1930s practice (Beall 1939), a persistence forecast that informed afternoon suppression planning.
- Introduction, "The Canadian Forest Service Fire Danger Group replaced the 1987 System, FWI1987, in 2025 with FWI2025." (qmd L2186-2225). The overview qualified the label, since Box 1 dated the "Fourth, current version of the FWI System" to 1984 (PDF p. 12, printed p. 6). The name FWI1987 therefore held as the year of Van Wagner's specification, not of the version.
- Introduction, "Its first listed key update was" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview added an earlier form of the change. In 2021 the daily codes were to use "daily maximum temperature, minimum humidity, and the wind speed at the time of maximum temperature" (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23). Hourly data could enter the calculation where available.
- Introduction, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported it, saying hourly methods "have been available to users for some time" (PDF p. 27, printed p. 21). It added that the disagreement among daily, hourly and diurnally adjusted codes was a long-standing user concern, and it did not mention Beck (1989).
- Introduction, "The revision changed the equations as well as the input." (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported it in general terms, saying the moisture calculation methods "will be modified" while the core structure stayed. It gave none of the specific changes, the halved time lag, the wind function or the rain event rule.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The Duff Moisture Code and the Drought Code dried only between sunrise and sunset" (qmd L2459-2468). The overview added the 2021 reason for an explicit day length, from latitude, longitude and calendar date in place of the month input (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23). Its one statement on day length and latitude concerned "the moisture codes" in general and named neither the Duff Moisture Code nor the Drought Code.
- Introduction, "Their rain thresholds of 1.5 mm and 2.8 mm were applied to rain accumulated within a rain event" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview added that rain timing was to become an optional input for "the new FWI System’s moisture content bookkeeping methods" (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23). It gave no thresholds and no rain event rule.
- Introduction, "The Buildup Index, the Fire Weather Index and the Daily Severity Rating equations" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported it in structure, since its Figure 5 core "does not change in structure from the current FWI System structure" (PDF p. 31, printed p. 25).
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1 nonetheless claimed continuity" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported continuity as a design aim from 2021, calling CFFDRS-2025 "simply as a further evolutionary step" (PDF p. 15, printed p. 9). It made no claim that values under the two versions would correspond.
- Introduction, "FWI1987 on its 13:00 observation against FWI2025 on hourly weather would confound the version change with the input change." (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported the reasoning, presenting the new input timing as the means of "reconciling differences between the daily FFMC (Van Wagner 1987) and hourly FFMC calculation methods" (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23). Version and input were planned as one change.
- Study Design, "Under pathway C the 13:00 observation was converted to estimated daily maxima and minima" (qmd L2469-2476). The overview supported the purpose of pathway C. It promised methods to adjust noon observations to daily extremes "for those who cannot access forecast maximums, and to adjust historical daily records" (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23).
- Seasonal Metrics, "The FWI2025 series were summarised to a daily value at 16:00 Local Standard Time" (qmd L2569). The overview added a different 2021 timing, placing "the final bookkeeping estimate of moisture content" at "the end of the drying period" (PDF p. 29, printed p. 23). That was late in the day or early the next morning, not a peak afternoon hour.
- Fire Danger Systems, "Its inputs were air temperature, relative humidity, wind speed at ten metres in the open and rainfall over the previous 24 hours" (qmd L2459-2468). The overview supported the four inputs, kept them while their timing changed, and referred to "the standard 10-m open wind observation" (PDF p. 33, printed p. 27).
- Introduction, "The Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC) tracks the surface litter" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported the three layers and gave the Duff Moisture Code layer as the forest floor's upper 0 to 7 cm.
- Fire Danger Systems, "The System rated the weather against one standard fuel" (qmd L2459-2468). The overview supported it, keeping closed canopy conifer as the reference fuel and describing the indices as indicators for "a typical jack pine stand" (PDF p. 30, printed p. 24).
- Fire Danger Systems, "The optional grassland codes that FWI2025 added" (qmd L2459-2468). The overview supported the exclusion, since it planned the grassland moisture, spread and fire weather indices and a Peatland Moisture Code as optional additions.
- Season Window, "It replaced the Canadian start rule, which began the calculation three days after the snow cover disappeared" (qmd L2551). The overview added that the start rule was itself under revision. It listed "Improved overwintering of drought and station start-up" (PDF p. 21, printed p. 15) and guidance on start dates from snow loss at the forest floor.
- Discussion, "The clearest difference between the two FWI2025 series" (qmd L3569). The overview supported the burning window as a planned output, promising guidance "on burning day length and overnight fire activity" (PDF p. 21, printed p. 15). Its glossary defined burn day length as hours, counted from about sunrise, when weather supported significant fire activity.
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence." (qmd L2186-2225). The overview added that Beall (1934) estimated fires by danger class from 14 years of Algonquin District records. It listed British Columbia occurrence models by Todd and Kourtz (1991), Kourtz and Todd (1991), Magnussen and Taylor (2012) and Nadeem et al. (2020).
- Introduction, "It also asked whether the relationship between danger and ignition was stationary" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview added a reason to expect drift, saying occurrence models "should be updated and refitted as new environmental data accrues" (PDF p. 43, printed p. 37).
- Introduction, table of expectations, row "Daily lightning, person-caused and all-cause ignitions", "weaker for lightning without strike data" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview supported the reasoning, since its national lightning models included observed strikes and a separate model used atmospheric stability as a proxy. It also named low station density as a limit on accuracy.
- Introduction, "Its terrain was steep enough that a station record was preferable to a reanalysis" (qmd L2186-2225). The overview added that the next generation would give "Guidance for using interpolation and spatial weather inputs (forecast, reanalysis, and ensembles)" (PDF p. 21, printed p. 15).
- Introduction, "No published study had compared the two on an observed record." (qmd L2186-2225). The overview was consistent with it, planning tests with early adopters but reporting none.
