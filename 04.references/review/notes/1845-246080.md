Subject. A tabular hourly grass moisture model for prescribed burning in Ontario tallgrass prairie, set beside the Fine Fuel Moisture Code it replaced.

Establishes. Kidnie, Wotton and Droog wrote the guide because fires in tallgrass consistently exceeded Fire Behaviour Prediction System forecasts for grass fuel types O-1a and O-1b. Grass was more exposed than the shaded pine litter of the Fine Fuel Moisture Code, and its response time was about one hour. The authors judged once daily indices of little value for grass, and found even the hourly code too slow. The guide therefore dropped the code and the Initial Spread Index for direct inputs of wind speed and fuel moisture. Moisture was tracked hour by hour through tables of solar radiation, fuel temperature and fuel humidity. Tracking began about four hours before a burn, and three hours were judged sufficient in most cases. Rain above 2 mm reset moisture to 250 per cent. The daily equilibrium moisture content was named the analogue of the daily code. The model came from matted grass, and its use for other Ontario grasses was untested. The guide held no evaluation of the Fire Weather Index System and no British Columbia data.

Quotations

"Actual fire behaviour in tallgrass prairies consistently exceeded Canadian Forest Fire Behaviour Prediction (FBP) predictions in the matted and standing grass fuel types (O-1a and O-1b) leading prescribed burn practitioners to under-predict expected fire behaviour as well as limiting the conditions under which tallgrass species can be expected to burn." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 1)

"The models used in this guide are not tallgrass fuel type specific and therefore the authors believe that this guide can be used for all grass types in Ontario, however this assumption has not been tested." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 1)

"Fuel layers in typical tallgrass sites are much more exposed than the shaded litter fuels modelled by the Canadian Forest Fire Weather Index (FWI) System’s Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC) and grass itself has a shorter response time that needle litter; response times in grasses have been found to be on the order of one hour." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 1)

"This increased exposure and lower characteristic response time leads to a very fast reacting fuel layer, one for which once daily moisture indices such as the FFMC are of little value. Indeed, even the hourly calculated FFMC reacts too slowly to track moisture content in exposed fields of grasses." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 1)

"If a reasonable estimate of starting moisture content can be made, tracking weather conditions and calculating fuel moisture content for the 3 hours prior to the burn should be sufficient in most cases to make a reasonable estimate of moisture content for the rate of spread prediction." (PDF p. 8, printed p. 1)

"The fuel moisture model used in this guide has been developed using observations from matted grasses." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2)

"The indices used in the FBP System, such as the Fine Fuel Moisture Content (FFMC) and Initial Spread Index (ISI), have been abandoned in favour of direct inputs such as wind speed and fuel moisture content." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2)

"The Equilibrium Moisture Content (EMC, see Appendix 9) is representative of the fuel moisture content of grasses during the peak burning period on days when weather is relatively stable and can be used as a surrogate for the FFMC in prescribed burn planning." (PDF p. 9, printed p. 2)

"In the new grass moisture model this value is essentially the analogue of the FWI System’s daily FFMC output." (PDF p. 12, printed p. 5)

"If rain exceeds 2 mm, all moisture contents go to 250%." (PDF p. 40, printed p. 33)
