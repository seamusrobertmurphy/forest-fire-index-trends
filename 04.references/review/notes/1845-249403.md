Subject. A history of the Canadian danger rating tables from 1916 to 1965 and a point by point comparison with the California system, written before the Fire Weather Index existed.

Establishes. The text traced the Canadian system from Wright's 1929 duff studies through the 1933, 1938, 1939, 1946 and 1950 tables. The 1933 index was computed in the evening and the 1938 hazard at 6:00 p.m. after evaporation had ceased. In 1939 tables first predicted the mid-afternoon tracer index from noon readings, evaporation was dropped because a noon reading could not give it, and a table approximated hazard at any hour from night wind. The 1938 tables were judged on fire occurrence by distance from the station, 96 per cent within 10 miles falling to 85 per cent at 60 miles. Beall's 1939 check found the drought measure very poor and listed six confounders of a fire incidence test. Macleod's 1948 night humidity study found corrections needed on 46 per cent of days in a dry season. California set its five classes by shares of days, 5, 45, 25, 20 and 5 per cent, and computed at 14:30. The text evaluated nothing itself and the first page of its reference list, items 1 to 13, is missing from the layer. The OCR carries heavy artefacts, reproduced as printed.

Bears on

- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. The noon observation dates from the 1939 tables, chosen so that the afternoon peak could be predicted about four hours ahead from the current day's weather, at the cost of dropping evaporation, which a noon reading could not give.
- Introduction, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds to it. A 1939 table approximated hazard at any time of day from night wind, and a night humidity correction from Macleod's 1948 study entered the 1950 tables, so sub daily adjustment is older than the hourly method.
- Introduction, "A danger rating system was judged on whether its outputs tracked fire occurrence" (qmd L2186-2225). Supports it. The 1938 tables were scored on fire occurrence by distance from the station, Beall checked the prairie tables in 1939 by fire frequency and size per hazard class, and the text stated the evaluation principle outright. It also listed six confounders of such a test, including suppression effort rising with danger.
- Introduction, "The Canadian Forest Service Fire Danger Group replaced the 1987 System" (qmd L2186-2225). Adds a precedent. In 1966 the 0 to 16 scale was judged too narrow and a 0 to 100 scale was planned, and any change to the tables required revising the whole system, the fault the modular Fire Weather Index later addressed.
- Methods, "Absolute thresholds applied unchanged to all three series" (qmd L2571). Adds to it. The Canadian classes rested on test fire behaviour while California set its classes as fixed shares of days, 5, 45, 25, 20 and 5 per cent, a percentile scheme that predates the manuscript's own.
- Discussion, "A daily index could not say when the peak arrived or how long it lasted" (qmd L3569). Supports it. The Canadian system gave the danger in the afternoon only, where the California system could be computed at any hour.
