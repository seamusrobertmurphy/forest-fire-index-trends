Subject. Three ways of computing an hourly Fine Fuel Moisture Code (FFMC) in Canada, compared on two weather series and by modelled fire growth for two 2007 fires.

Establishes. In 2009, Kerry Anderson of the Canadian Forest Service compared three ways of computing an hourly FFMC. The hourly method, labelled CVW, was Van Wagner's of 1977, which by 2009 used the moisture scale of the 1987 daily equations. The diurnal method, labelled BDL, read hourly values from the tables of Lawson and others (1996), projected from the noon daily FFMC without hourly weather. The third, labelled EMC, set the fuel moisture of each hour to its equilibrium moisture content, which assumed instantaneous drying and wetting. Rain was left out of all three. The first test ran four days of hourly weather built from daily extremes with Beck and Trevitt (1989), for the climate and latitude of Petawawa. Temperature ran from 10 to 25 °C and wind from 5 to 10 km/h. The minimum relative humidity was 30 per cent, with vapour pressure held constant. BDL ranged from 72.7 near dawn to 91.5 in the afternoon, CVW from 86.4 to 90.6 and EMC from 81.8 to 92.0. The Figure 4 legend gave the predicted four-day fire size in uniform C-2 boreal spruce fuel. It was 88 411 ha with CVW, 65 238 ha with EMC and 39 441 ha with BDL. The second test used observed hourly weather at Fort Smith, Northwest Territories, from June 1 to 4, 2007. Temperature ran from 10 to 27 °C, humidity from 20 to 81 per cent and wind from calm to 22.2 km/h. CVW again varied much less than the other two, and EMC closely followed BDL. Predicted area burned was 97 242 ha with BDL, 164 561 ha with EMC and 233 588 ha with CVW. The third test ran 54 fire simulation days from May 30 to June 28, 2007, for fires 07WB001 and 07WB002 in Wood Buffalo National Park. The two fires burned more than 200 000 ha from May into July. The CVW and EMC results were those of Anderson and others (2009), and the BDL results were added. Bias was the ratio of the sums of predicted and observed area burned, with observed growth taken from satellite hotspots. For the two fires, bias was 1.33 and 1.11 with BDL, 1.70 and 1.49 with EMC, and 2.05 and 1.78 with CVW. Setting the FFMC to zero between sunset and sunrise lowered these to 1.23 and 1.01, 1.51 and 1.31, and 1.51 and 1.29. Anderson judged by eye that daytime values of the three methods agreed closely, and that BDL presumably better reflected the overnight range. He concluded that CVW did not produce the expected daily range of the FFMC and that radiation was missing from CVW and EMC. BDL dropped 2 to 4 points at the change from the 11:00 projection to the noon observation. The text compared no FFMC with measured fuel moisture. It did not state whether the 54 simulation days ran on observed or forecast weather. Its acknowledgements thanked the Canadian Meteorological Centre for forecast weather.

Bears on

- Introduction, "An hourly method for the Fine Fuel Moisture Code had existed since @vanwagner1977hourly, and a sinusoidal method for expanding daily extremes into an hourly weather stream since @beck1989diurnal." (qmd L2188). Adds to it. The text dated the hourly method to Van Wagner's report, "In 1977, Van Wagner published an hourly version of the calculations, referred to as the hourly FFMC" (PDF p. 2, no printed page number). By 2009, that method had taken the 1987 daily moisture scale, which "is used in the current version of the hourly calculation" (PDF p. 2). The text built its hourly test weather from daily extremes, stating "The derived diurnal cycle was based on trends developed by Beck and Trevitt (1989)" (PDF p. 3). It named alpha, beta and gamma terms but never called the curve sinusoidal, so that word rests on another source.
- Introduction, "The report stated that hourly calculation allowed it to 'eliminate the differences that arise in the values of the daily FFMC, the hourly FFMC and the diurnally adjusted FFMC' [@cffdg2025fwi2025, p. 40]. The diurnally adjusted code came from look-up tables that gave a Fine Fuel Moisture Code 'for each hour around the clock without the need for hourly weather data' [@lawson2008weather, p. 4]." (qmd L2188). Adds to it. In 2009, the text measured those differences on one weather cycle, where "Values based on Lawson et al. show the largest degree of variation between the peak values of 91.5 in the afternoon and the lowest value 72.7 near dawn" (PDF p. 3). On the same cycle, Van Wagner's hourly FFMC ran only from 86.4 to 90.6 (PDF p. 3). It described the tables as the manuscript does, "in the diurnal method the only weather used is the noon weather conditions to calculate the standard daily FFMC" (PDF p. 6). Its next sentence added "(allowance is made for humidity conditions the next morning)" (PDF p. 6), so the tables took no hourly weather but did allow for next morning humidity.
- Introduction, "The report compared the Fine Fuel Moisture Code of each version with field samples on four days of June 1999 in the Northwest Territories [@cffdg2025fwi2025, pp. 19–20]. No published study had compared the two versions over a longer observed record." (qmd L2200). Qualifies it, without contradicting it. The text predated FWI2025 and compared neither version as a whole. It did compare the 1977 hourly method, which FWI2025 later modified (GLC-X-42, PDF p. 24, printed p. 18), with diurnal tables built on the noon daily FFMC. Its observed weather record was "a four-day period from June 1 to 4, 2007" at Fort Smith (PDF p. 3), no longer than the report's four days. Its longest test, "54 fire simulation days covering the period from May 30 to June 28, 2007" (PDF p. 4), judged modelled fire growth against hotspots, not the FFMC itself. It compared no FFMC with field samples, and cited Beck and Armitage (2004) for samples from the International Crown Fire Modelling Experiment (PDF p. 6). The report's Figures 7 and 8 drew their samples from the same experiment (GLC-X-42, PDF pp. 25 and 26, printed pp. 19 and 20). The sentence therefore holds only because it names the two versions, since hourly and noon-based FFMC methods had been compared on observed weather in 2009.
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1.1 stated that, 'to better match field observations of litter moisture content variation throughout the day, the time lag function used in the FWI2025 for the FFMC has been cut in half' [@cffdg2025fwi2025, p. 19]." (qmd L2188). Adds to it. In 2009, the text found that Van Wagner's hourly method "does not produce the expected range of diurnal values of the FFMC" (PDF p. 6). Setting the moisture content to equilibrium "produces diurnal variations closer to expected values", and over-prediction of fire growth fell by 30 per cent (PDF p. 6). The report's drying paragraph cited Beck and Armitage 2004, Wotton 2007, H. E. Anderson 1990, Nelson 2001 and Van Wagner 1979, but not this paper (GLC-X-42, PDF p. 25, printed p. 19).
- Introduction, "This meant that 'an FFMC of 91 in FWI1987 should correspond to an FFMC of approximately 91 in FWI2025' [@cffdg2025fwi2025, p. 18]." (qmd L2200). Adds to it, weakly. By eye, the text found "the daytime FFMCs of all three models in relatively close agreement" (PDF p. 6), with the differences lying overnight. Its hourly method kept the 1977 drying rate that FWI2025 later halved, so the agreement need not carry over to FWI2025.
