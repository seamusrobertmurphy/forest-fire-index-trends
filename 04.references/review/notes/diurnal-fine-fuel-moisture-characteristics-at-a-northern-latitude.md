Subject. During 1999 and 2000, in the Northwest Territories, the authors tested the hourly and the diurnally adjusted Fine Fuel Moisture Codes against fine fuels sampled hourly.

Establishes. During 1999 and 2000, the authors sampled fuel moisture at two stands beside the burn plots of the International Crown Fire Modelling Experiment (printed p. 213). The site lay about 50 km north of Fort Providence, Northwest Territories, at 61°35′N, 117°10′W (printed p. 213). Both stands had a jack pine and black spruce overstory, and only Site 1 had a black spruce understory, of 2,350 stems per hectare (Table 1, printed p. 213). Canopy cover was 61 per cent at Site 1 and 59 per cent at Site 2, with jack pine at 2,489 and 2,438 stems per hectare (Table 1). Samples were taken every hour from about 05:00 to 22:00 on 17 days, between 19 June and 2 July 1999 and between 15 and 26 June 2000 (printed p. 214). Each hour, 20 to 50 g each of needles, feathermoss tips, twigs of two sizes and black spruce bark flakes were collected, about 200 sets a site (printed p. 214). A forest floor sample from 0 to 2 cm was added, and moisture was taken as a percentage of oven-dry weight after drying at 100 °C (printed p. 214). An automatic weather station in the open recorded temperature, relative humidity, wind and rain hourly, with a rain gauge precise to 0.25 mm (printed p. 213). From those observations, hourly codes were computed by the hourly model of Van Wagner (1977) and by the diurnal adjustments of Lawson et al. (1996) (printed p. 214). The diurnal method adjusted the current day's standard code for 13:00 to 06:59 daylight time, and the previous day's code with relative humidity for 07:00 to 12:59 (printed p. 219). Each code was converted to moisture content with Van Wagner's (1987) equation for pine needle litter, 147.2(101 − FFMC)/(59.5 + FFMC), the text's Equation 1 (printed p. 214). On dry days, measured needle and feathermoss moisture fell to a late afternoon minimum and rose quickly to an overnight maximum (printed p. 217). Between 17:00 on 19 June and 06:00 on 20 June, the diurnal model gave 6 to 24 per cent and the hourly model 6 to 12 per cent (printed p. 217). Neither the text nor the Figure 2 caption gave the year of those two days. Measured moisture over the same hours ran from 4 to 18 per cent in needle litter and 4 to 24 per cent in feathermoss (printed p. 218). As drawn in Figure 2a, the hourly line appeared to peak nearer 14 or 15 per cent than 12, judged by eye (printed p. 216). Both models overestimated the dry-day minimum in both fuels (printed p. 218). The authors judged that the diurnal model matched the feathermoss amplitude best and the hourly model the needle amplitude (printed pp. 211, 218). By the text's own example, the needle range of 14 percentage points was nearer the diurnal model's 18 than the hourly model's 6. On dry days, both fuels appeared to lose and gain moisture faster than the hourly model allowed, so its rates were too slow in both directions (printed p. 219). The authors added that more work was needed to quantify those rates, and called for the hourly model to be studied and possibly refitted (printed pp. 219, 220). They attributed the misfit between measured and modelled moisture to Equation 1, which they judged not strictly applicable to their fuels (printed pp. 218, 219). On printed p. 219 the text paired the hourly code with lodgepole pine needle litter, against its own pages 212 and 218, which gave jack pine needle trays. Equation 1 slightly overestimated needle moisture and greatly underestimated feathermoss moisture, though the differences became small above a code of 77 (Figure 4, printed p. 218). After 9.12 mm of rain on 22 June 1999, the hourly model responded quickly and tracked the next day's drying reasonably well (Figure 3, printed pp. 217, 219). Following rain, both models overestimated needle moisture and underestimated feathermoss moisture, and the diurnal model could not account for rain (printed p. 219). The diurnal model was also discontinuous at its 07:00 and 13:00 transitions, most visibly after rain (printed p. 219). In Table 3, needle litter averaged 21.79 per cent, SD 17.94, at Site 1 and 22.17 per cent, SD 18.76, at Site 2 (printed p. 215). Feathermoss tips averaged 75.62 per cent, SD 79.92, and 87.95 per cent, SD 95.27, with maxima of 363.46 and 386.67 per cent (Table 3). Moisture differed between sites only in duff and bark flakes, by Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (printed pp. 215, 219). Within-stand records of 2000, 192 at each site, ran from about 0 to 32 °C and 15 to 89 per cent relative humidity (Table 2, printed p. 215). The authors did not test how the differences between the codes would change predicted fire behaviour (printed p. 219). The citation printed on PDF page 1 matched the one supplied, and PDF pages 1 to 11 carried printed pages 211 to 221.

Bears on

- Introduction, "From one observation a day, taken at 13:00 local daylight time," (qmd L2188). Adds to it, since the text stated that the 13:00 daily code was meant to depict fine fuel moisture at about 17:00 daylight time, the peak of burning (printed p. 212).
- Introduction, "of a standard pine stand" (qmd L2188). Qualifies it, since the hourly code was checked on jack pine needles in trays and the diurnal tables on lodgepole pine needles with feathermoss and lichen tips (printed pp. 212, 218).
- Introduction, "The report stated that hourly calculation allowed it to" (qmd L2188). Adds to it, since the text measured those differences on a dry day and traced them partly to different reference fuels behind each code (printed pp. 217, 219).
- Introduction, "The diurnally adjusted code came from look-up tables" (qmd L2188). Qualifies it, since the tables came from a site near Prince George at 54°22′N, ignored afternoon rain and needed relative humidity for morning hours (printed pp. 212, 219).
- Introduction, "The report's section 4.1.1 stated that," (qmd L2188). Adds to it, since hourly samples at 61°35′N showed needles and feathermoss losing and gaining moisture faster than the 1977 hourly code, the direction of the 2025 change (printed p. 219).
- Introduction, "The time lag is the time the litter takes" (qmd L2188). Qualifies it, since the text put the share lost at 1 − 1/e, about two-thirds, of free moisture above equilibrium (printed p. 214).
- Introduction, "The report compared the Fine Fuel Moisture Code of each version with field samples" (qmd L2200). Adds to it, since this text matched the report's four days in place, month, year and purpose (printed pp. 211, 213). Its 1999 sampling ran from 19 June to 2 July and assessed the hourly and diurnal codes (printed pp. 213, 214). It cannot show that the report used these data, which needs the report's own source line or the later paper. Its one dated rain, 9.12 mm, fell on 22 June 1999 (Figure 3, printed p. 217).
- Methods, "Its drying time lag was halved," (qmd L2466). Qualifies it, since the fuels gained as well as lost moisture faster than the hourly code, so report p. 19 should be checked for the wetting rate (printed p. 219).
- Methods, "Canopy interception of the first 0.5 mm of rain became cumulative" (qmd L2466). Adds to it, since the text stated that the hourly code did not exclude the first 0.5 mm, which the daily code ignored over 24 h (printed p. 212). It added that hourly rain effects had yet to be field validated.
- Discussion, "It was not to be expected at a site with a different diurnal range," (qmd L3562). Adds to it, since the field test sat at 61°35′N in late June, where the text noted lower overnight humidity and higher overnight temperature than at lower latitudes (printed p. 212).
- Discussion, "The version change halved the fine fuel time lag." (qmd L3564). Qualifies it, since the text reported Van Wagner's hourly rates as "one-eighth of the standard daily rates", so the 2025 halving applied to an hourly code already faster than FWI1987's daily code (printed p. 212).
- Discussion, "That was consistent with a 16:00 daily value taken nearer the afternoon peak" (qmd L3566). Qualifies it, since FWI1987's daily code was itself meant to depict fine fuel moisture at about 17:00 daylight time, which is 16:00 standard time (printed p. 212).
