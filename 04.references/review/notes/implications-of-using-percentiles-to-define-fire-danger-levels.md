Subject. A 2009 handout on how the percentile breakpoints of the United States National Fire Danger Rating System moved with the station, years, months, missing data and fuel model used.

Establishes. In the United States National Fire Danger Rating System (NFDRS), the Very High and Extreme classes most commonly began at the 90th and 97th percentiles of an index. The weather station manager set those breakpoints, and the station catalog did not record the years and months behind them. The handout examined how those levels moved at eight Remote Automated Weather Stations, with records of 6 to 39 years ending in 2008, computed in FireFamilyPlus 4.0. Over 1978 to 1987, the Energy Release Component (ERC) had a 90th percentile of 53 at both Black Creek, Mississippi, and Missoula, Montana, under fuel models D and G. The handout wrote the fuel model after the index, so ERC(G) was the Energy Release Component under fuel model G. Very High and Extreme held 7 and 3 per cent of days at both stations by definition. The lower classes were set as fractions of the 90th percentile value, and High held 40 per cent of days at Black Creek against 64 per cent at Missoula. At Missoula, breakpoints set from 1999 to 2008 placed 27 days of 2000 in Very High under the 90th and 97th percentile standard and 56 under the 80th and 95th. Those breakpoints were ERC(G) 58 and 69 under the first standard and 47 and 66 under the second. Under fuel models G and H over 1999 to 2008, the 97th percentile was ERC 69 and 39 at Missoula, and Burning Index (BI) 42 and 18 at Conroe, Texas. Daily values under the two models were close to linear in each other, with coefficients of determination of 0.9654 and 0.9326. Classes set from each model's own distribution held a similar number of days, with 7 and 3 per cent in Very High and Extreme under both. At Tower, New Mexico, and at Missoula, limits set over 2003 to 2008 from fire season months were higher than limits set from the whole year. At Missoula, ERC(G) Very High began at 58 from May to October data and at 47 from January to December data, and Extreme at 70 and 66. In May to October 2000, the seasonal and annual limits gave 45 and 75 Very High days and 12 and 26 Extreme days. At Tower, ERC(U) limits from April to September and from the whole year were 57 and 54 for Very High and 66 and 59 for Extreme. In April to September 2004, they gave 14 and 26 Very High days and 4 and 10 Extreme days. The authors warned that fire season data could be biased without a consistent definition of the season, and that the season might be lengthening. Year-round records ending in 2008 over 10, 20, 25, 30 and 39 years gave 90th percentiles of BI(B) at Mt. Laguna, California, from 119 to 129. Its 97th percentile ran from 138 to 145. At Missoula, ERC(G) ran from 52 to 58 at the 90th percentile and from 66 to 69 at the 97th. The ten year base of 1999 to 2008 gave the highest values at both stations. A 30 year base was within 1 unit of the 39 year base at the 90th percentile at both. The station catalogs held 114 and 148 at Mt. Laguna and 55 and 62 at Missoula, matching none of these values. At Missoula, year-round curves were alike across base lengths, but June to August curves differed, because the early record held data mainly in June to August. The authors read this as missing data that might mask change in fire season weather. Many fire weather stations, they wrote, began consistent year-round collection only in the 1990s. Over six overlapping ten year windows of June to October, the 90th percentile of BI(B) at Mt. Laguna was 111 for 1974 to 1983 and 86 for 1979 to 1988. It then rose to 129 for 1999 to 2008, and the 97th percentile rose from 119 for 1984 to 1993 to 145. The authors left open whether this was climate change or the El Niño-Southern Oscillation. For a warming station, they suggested counting more than 3 per cent of days as Extreme under current conditions rather than updating the thresholds. They concluded that the 97th percentile value depended on the years and months used, and that missing data could have a large and sometimes hidden effect. They wrote that the 97th percentile standard was not necessarily correlated with fire occurrence or size. They called for the method and dataset to be recorded, and for guidelines on how many years to use and how often to update thresholds. The handout fitted no trend, said nothing of the Canadian Fire Weather Index System and did not address the bias between counts inside and outside a base period. Three printed results did not agree with the rest of the handout. The text put 14 extra Extreme days at Missoula under seasonal data, but Table 5 gave the larger count, 26 days, to the lower annual limit. The text called the 30 year base 1989 to 2008, but Table 6 printed its 119 against 1979 to 2008. Table 7b printed June to August ERC(G) values at Missoula of 38 to 47 at the 90th percentile and 50 to 66 at the 97th. Read from a 600 dpi image, no curve in Figure 7b crossed the 90th percentile below ERC 45. The pair 47 and 66 also appeared as Missoula's 1999 to 2008 80th and 95th percentiles in Figure 2a and as its annual 2003 to 2008 limits in Table 5. The Missoula values of Table 7b should not be cited as printed.

Quotations

"Interpretation of indices is based on climatology, using selected years and months. Climatology-based percentile levels are used to define the average and extreme conditions for an area." (PDF p. 1, handout)

"The weather station manager specifies the top two breakpoints. While these breakpoints may be documented in the local fire management plan, there is no formal documentation in the station catalog of the data (years, months) used to set those breakpoints." (PDF p. 1, handout)

"The 90th percentile ERC value for both sites is 53. While the breakpoints for the lower classes are the same (13 and 27), the percent of days in the classes are quite different (Fig. 1c; Table 3). The “High” class includes 40% of the days for MS and 64% for MT, a difference of 24%." (PDF p. 1, handout)

"Depending on the standard used to set the classes, there would have been either 27 or 56 days in the “Very High” class in 2000, a difference of 29 days (Fig. 2b). Analysts comparing the number of days with fire danger in the highest classes should be aware of the method used to set the levels." (PDF p. 2, handout)

"The 97th percentile for MT is 69 for ERC(G) and 39 for ERC(H), while the 97th percentile for TX is 42 for BI(G) and 18 for BI(H). When the indices are interpreted as fire danger levels based on percentiles, a similar number of days fall in each class (Fig. 3d). As a relative indicator of fire danger, there is essentially no benefit of using fuel model H over fuel model G." (PDF p. 4, handout)

"The “Extreme” class in MT has ERC ≥ 70 based on weather data (2003-2008) taken during the fire season, and ERC ≥ 66 based on year-round data, resulting in 14 more days in the “Extreme” class when using seasonal data (Table 5). The “Very High” class contains 30 more days using annual data because of the lower calculated threshold value." (PDF p. 6, handout)

"Caution should be used in using annual data, unless an area has year-round fire activity. The “Very High” and “Extreme” danger rating thresholds may be lower than warranted when including non-fire season months with low fire danger index values. If annual data are used, annual data should be available for every year in the selected date range to avoid hidden biases. In addition, use of fire season data can be biased without a consistent definition of the “fire season.” Additionally, the length of the fire season may be increasing; the definition of a “fire season” should be sensitive to such changes." (PDF p. 6, handout)

"In addition to deciding which months to use in setting fire danger levels, the number of years of data used, which years are used, and how often results should be updated must be determined. Climate change and missing data are important considerations as well. There are not yet agreed-upon answers." (PDF p. 6, handout)

"Longer-term datasets provide more information on a site’s historic climatology, but many fire weather stations did not begin consistent year-round data collection until the 1990s. Determining which data are available ensures that results are not skewed by months of missing data." (PDF p. 6, handout)

"In each case a percentile curve is plotted for 10, 20, 25, 30, and 39 years of weather data, all ending in 2008, illustrating that the number of years of data used can impact results. There is a large difference in the results for CA (Fig. 6a). The results for the last 30 years (1989-2008) and the last ten years (1999-2008) form the extremes, with 90th percentile values of 119 and 129, respectively." (PDF p. 7, handout)

"The MT curves, on the other hand, appear quite similar for each time period when year-round data are used. The results are different when only June-August data (Fig. 6c) are used because in the early record, data exist primarily during June-August, suggesting that missing data may be masking changes in weather conditions during the fire season." (PDF p. 7, handout)

"It is worthwhile for a weather station manager to perform a periodic analysis of the threshold values in the Station Catalog. This is not to suggest that previously calculated values are invalid, merely that conditions that used to be rare (3% of the time) may now be more common, especially as the effects of climate change increase." (PDF pp. 7-8, handout)

"Notice that for the CA site, index values have been increasing since the 1979-1988 period. More research is needed to distinguish recent climate change signals from those caused by climate oscillations such as the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO)." (PDF p. 8, handout)

"Use of climatology is required for proper interpretation of fire danger indices and for development of fire danger classes. Percentiles normalize indices across broad geographic areas, allowing intercomparison of indices, fuel models, and model inputs (e.g., Slope Class)." (PDF p. 9, handout)

"A commonly used standard for defining extreme fire danger is the 97th percentile. However, this standard is not necessarily correlated with fire occurrence or size. The value associated with the 97th percentile varies depending on the years and months of data included in the analysis. The results of using annual data may be much different than those using fire season data. Missing data can have a large, sometimes hidden, impact on results." (PDF p. 9, handout)

"For stations in areas with increasing temperatures, the 97th percentile value can be lower for a prior 10-year period than for the most recent 10-year period. Rather than updating fire danger thresholds, it may be important to recognize that, under current conditions, more than 3% of days should now be classified as “Extreme.”" (PDF p. 10, handout)

"Complete documentation will aid application and communication of fire danger. The method and dataset used to set levels could be added to the Station Catalog." (PDF p. 10, handout)

"Guidelines are needed for how many years of data to use and how often to update thresholds. An assessment is needed to guide fire managers in using fire danger to assess climate change impacts." (PDF p. 12, handout)

"Fire danger levels based on percentiles depend only on historical weather data for selected years and season." (symposium abstract page)

Bears on

- Methods, Seasonal Metrics, "They were the 90th and 95th percentiles of each series' own Fire Weather Index distribution, and the 95th percentile of its Initial Spread Index distribution." (qmd L2574). Adds to it. The handout supported thresholds from each station's own distribution, since "Percentiles normalize indices across broad geographic areas, allowing intercomparison of indices, fuel models, and model inputs (e.g., Slope Class)." (PDF p. 9, handout). It also warned that "The value associated with the 97th percentile varies depending on the years and months of data included in the analysis." (PDF p. 9, handout). The fixed 1991 to 2020 base and the calendar window at qmd L2552 met that warning. In Table 6, a 30 year base was within 1 unit of the 39 year base at the 90th percentile at both stations (PDF p. 9, handout). A 10 year base departed by 9 at Mt. Laguna and 4 at Missoula. Each station's base held only its kept seasons, so a record length effect applied between stations, while the three series at one station shared one record.
- Discussion, Published Trends, "A series of percentile exceedances was unaffected by the version change, because the threshold moved with the distribution." (qmd L3576). Adds to it. The nearest case in the handout was a change of fuel model, not of system version. Over 1999 to 2008, the 97th percentile was ERC 69 under fuel model G and 39 under H at Missoula, and BI 42 and 18 at Conroe (PDF p. 4, handout). The handout found that "When the indices are interpreted as fire danger levels based on percentiles, a similar number of days fall in each class (Fig. 3d)." (PDF p. 4, handout). Daily values under the two models were close to linear in each other, with coefficients of determination of 0.9654 and 0.9326 (PDF p. 5, Figure 3b). The equal shares in Very High and Extreme held over the base years by definition, and the handout did not compare counts year by year.
- Discussion, Published Trends, "A percentile trend was portable across the version change only when its reference period was stated, kept fixed and corrected for the base-period bias." (qmd L3576). Qualifies it. The handout supported stating the method, since "Analysts comparing the number of days with fire danger in the highest classes should be aware of the method used to set the levels." (PDF p. 2, handout). It supported a fixed threshold, since "Rather than updating fire danger thresholds, it may be important to recognize that, under current conditions, more than 3% of days should now be classified as “Extreme.”" (PDF p. 10, handout). It added two conditions the sentence omitted, the months that formed the base and complete data inside it. Over 2003 to 2008, the Missoula Extreme limit was ERC(G) 70 from May to October data and 66 from year-round data (PDF p. 6, handout). The authors also found that "Missing data can have a large, sometimes hidden, impact on results." (PDF p. 9, handout). The calendar window at qmd L2552 and the 95 per cent coverage rule met both conditions, but the sentence did not name them. The handout did not address the base-period bias.
